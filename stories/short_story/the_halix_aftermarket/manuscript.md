# The Halix Aftermarket

## I. The Empty Stone

The stone arrived in a seller's case, which was the first discourtesy.

Tess Vlin had finished cleaning the brass taste from her tuning ring and was balancing the
fourth-deck receipts when the courier set the case on her counter. It was black polymer,
commercial grade, with a latch calibrated for bulk reliquaries. A family would have used
wood. A hospice would have used unbleached linen. A clade would have used whatever had
held the stone before it became precious, because Drift Clades distrusted containers that
improved themselves after grief.

This case wanted to look neutral.

Tess looked at the courier.

He was Drift Clade, young, long-boned in the way of low-gravity children who had learned
to apologize with posture. He kept both hands on the counter after releasing the case, as if
someone had told him not to touch his pockets while being evaluated.

"Inheritance quarter," he said. "Tess Vlin's bench."

"You found it."

"Good." He swallowed. "I was told you would know the difference."

The case hummed no note Tess could hear. That was possible. Some stones rested quietly
until ringed. Others had been over-wrapped for transport. She did not open it.

"Between what and what?"

He took a folded slip from his sleeve and placed it beside the case. "Between something
finished and something dead."

That was a better answer than most couriers brought to Halix.

Tess unfolded the slip.

The handwriting was clade-plain, spare and vertical. Donor name. Holder name. Intended
hearer. Three people, all related by one missed generation. Date of tuning. Date of hearing.
Witness mark from a pod elder in the outer sleeve. No Archivist seal. No Solace seal. No
Consortium seal, which improved the paper at once.

Purpose requested: valuation, then open sale if viable.

Tess read the line again.

"Who sent you?"

"My aunt."

"Is she the hearer?"

"No. The hearer stopped last season. The stone was on her shelf. The frame needs work."

There it was. Halix's oldest sentence, wearing a new coat: the walls need memory more
than the dead need dignity. Tess did not despise the sentence. She had made a living from
its grammar.

She set the slip down. "You understand what this claims to be."

"A closed Light-Bequest."

He said it carefully, capital letters audible in the mouth. Someone had coached him. Good.
The uncoached were too easy to rob.

"Most closed Light-Bequests do not come to market," Tess said.

"Most pods have better frames."

Also a good answer.

Tess slid the case toward herself but still did not open it. Three booths down, a civic-memory
broker was shouting over a disputed foundation song. Two booths beyond that, someone had
lowered a ring badly and the air had gone sour with a childhood kitchen being forced to
perform for strangers. Halix Exchange breathed around them: hot oil from the lower decks,
incense from the apology stalls, ozone from too many reliquary cradles working in too little
space. Four kilometers of ship, market, chapel, and knife.

"If I open this," Tess said, "I am not promising sale."

"My aunt said you would say that."

"Your aunt sounds expensive."

"She says the same about you."

Tess allowed him one small corner of a smile. "Sit."

He sat.

She unlatched the case.

The stone inside was the size of a thumb joint, pale gray, polished from long handling
rather than from bench vanity. It rested in a nest of felt too new for it. The felt was another
discourtesy. The stone had known older cloth. Tess could see the absent shape of it in the
surface: oils from a hand, a small crescent where a holder's nail had worried the same edge
for years.

She did not touch it directly. She lifted the case insert, set it in the cradle, and lowered the
tuning ring.

The booth thickened.

With an open Light-Bequest, the first register rose like a hand from water: donor voice,
single locus, a memory intended for someone who had not existed yet. The second register
followed underneath, the hearer-shaped absence. Tess had heard one cleanly not long ago.
It had left the air around her bench tender for the rest of the watch.

This stone did not sing.

It did not fail, either. A failed stone scraped. A counterfeit stone over-declared itself. A
stone that had been coerced kept a cut note no polishing could entirely hide. This one gave
Tess none of those mercies.

It was quiet with structure.

Under the ring, the booth held a small pressure, neat as a folded sheet. No voice. No hollow.
No waiting. The stone had the density of a door after the person you loved had already
passed through it and closed it behind them.

Tess lifted the ring.

The booth thinned. Noise returned: bargaining, prayer, a child laughing too close to a
counter where laughter should have been kept brief.

The courier had gone very still.

"Well?" he asked.

"It is closed."

His face changed, but only at the edges. Relief first. Then calculation. Then the shame of
calculation arriving before grief had finished with the room.

"So it is real."

"Yes."

"And viable?"

Tess looked down at the stone.

This was the sentence around which the market had built itself: every object has a price if
the need on both sides is sufficient. Tess had believed it most days. On the other days, she
had believed it anyway and charged less.

"It has no usable memory left," she said. "It will not power a drive. It will not stabilize a
frame. It will not comfort the dying. The intended hearing has already happened. The donor
memory is no longer in the stone in any form I can honestly value."

"Then it is dead."

"No."

The answer came too quickly. Tess did not like that. Quick answers usually belonged to
doctrine.

She steadied her hands on the counter. "It is finished."

The courier looked at the stone as if it had tricked him by being accurate.

"What is finished worth?"

Tess wished, briefly and with professional sincerity, that he had taken the case to Solace.
Solace would have given him tea, doctrine, and a refusal shaped like mercy. Halix gave
numbers. Numbers were crueler only because they were easier to carry home.

"Let me enter the assay," she said.

He nodded.

That was when the market noticed.

## II. Provenance

Halix did not have bells. Bells were for monasteries and ships that trusted sound not to
become panic. Halix had ledgers.

When Tess entered a lot classification, the bench sent a narrow pulse through the fourth
deck's trading mesh: type, status, restrictions, provisional value. Most pulses vanished into
the day's silt. Personal stone, minor, stable. Civic fragment, disputed. Hospice comfort,
sealed. Fraud, polite removal requested.

Tess typed: Light-Bequest, closed.

The mesh held the words for half a breath.

Then her counter received seven inquiries.

The courier saw the numbers flare along the bench glass. His eyes widened.

"You said it had no usable memory."

"It doesn't."

"Then why?"

"Because I used the wrong word in a hungry room."

Tess muted the inquiries with the side of her hand. More arrived. A private cabinet buyer.
A lattice archive office. Two brokers she did not trust. One cultural program with no name
attached, which meant Consortium money had learned a little shame. A Drift Clade agent
asking whether the family had release rights or sale rights. That one she left unread. Clade
families could do their own wounding without Halix improving the blade.

The courier's face was open now, young enough that greed and hope had not learned
separate masks.

"There is a market," he said.

"There is attention."

"Isn't that the same thing here?"

Tess looked at the stone.

The terrible part was that he was not entirely wrong. Memory generated coherence. That
was not poetry. Everyone with a tuning bench knew it in the joints. The stone itself held no
voice, no waiting hollow, no inheritance still in transit. But the idea of it - a completed
gift, verified, portable - had weight. The buyers were not bidding on memory. They were
bidding on the memory of memory having done its work.

The cradle scale registered a fractional rise.

Tess stared at it.

The stone had not changed. The room had.

Three people had glanced toward her booth. Five now. The ledger mesh thickened around
the classification. Halix, which remembered profit with civic devotion, had begun to
remember the stone as a lot.

"Do not touch the case," Tess said.

The courier pulled his hands back.

The first buyer arrived with no hurry. Private cabinet trade, by the coat: soft black, cuffs
stitched with conductive thread, hair arranged to imply he had never leaned over a dying
bed. He stopped at the courtesy mark outside Tess's counter and smiled at the courier as
though the courier were furniture with legal standing.

"Tess," he said. "I saw an unusual classification."

"Then your eyes work."

"Closed Light-Bequest. Verified?"

"Provisional."

"May I see the witness slip?"

"No."

His smile thinned. "I buy provenance, not secrets."

"On Halix those are cousins."

He accepted that with a small tilt of the head. "For a private devotional cabinet. No burn,
no assay beyond your seal, no public play. The buyer collects completed inheritances."

"There are not enough completed inheritances to collect."

"There will be, once families learn there is a dignified afterlife for the carriers."

Tess heard the shape of it at once: velvet shelves, low light, grieving visitors paying to
stand before stones that had been emptied by other people's love. Finished objects turned
into invitations. Sacredness with an admission schedule.

"No viewing rights," she said.

"Then not a cabinet sale."

"Correct."

He looked at the courier. "Your family should hear the number before your broker grows
protective."

Tess named a number herself. Low. Accurate to material, workmanship, and carrier
integrity. The cabinet buyer laughed once, softly, because he thought she had made a joke.
Then he named ten times that.

The courier gripped the edge of his chair.

Tess did not look at him. "Offer noted."

"Offer expires when I leave the quarter."

"Then walk slowly."

He did not leave. Of course he did not. Halix buyers liked to watch each other bleed.

The second buyer came in gray.

Not the same Consortium fixer who had stood at Tess's bench for a different Light-Bequest
and tried to turn a grandmother's voice into civic reinforcement. This one was a woman,
older, with a clean face and no jewelry except a ring that was probably a recorder. The
Consortium trained its people to look kind without looking soft. It saved time.

"Vlin," she said.

"I do not remember inviting the Lattice to my counter."

"No one remembers inviting the Lattice anywhere. Yet infrastructure persists."

Tess disliked her immediately, which was unfair. Accuracy was not arrogance simply
because it arrived in gray.

The woman looked at the courier. "I will make my interest plain. We are not seeking fuel.
We are not seeking donor content. We want the carrier, the assay record, and the witness
chain. A closed Light-Bequest with clean provenance is rare enough to be instructive."

"Instructive for what?"

"Public trust."

The cabinet buyer made a small bored sound. The Consortium woman ignored him.

"Families resist Light-Bequests because they fear incompletion," she said. "They fear the
stone will hold grief indefinitely, or that the intended hearer will mishandle it, or that
closure will damage the donor trace. A verified closed specimen proves otherwise."

The courier looked at Tess. "Is that true?"

Tess hated him a little for asking in front of them. She hated herself more for knowing the
answer mattered.

"It proves one stone completed cleanly," she said.

"One is how public confidence begins," the woman said. "We can preserve the names."

"You can preserve anything except scale."

"Scale saves lives."

"Scale sands edges."

The Consortium woman accepted the rebuke without visible injury. That, too, was training.

"My offer is for acquisition, not display," she said. "The family may retain a redacted copy
of the provenance chain. We will also pay for a full coherence-frame retuning on the pod
of origin, administered by a licensed contractor."

The courier made a small sound in his throat.

There it was: not greed now. Relief sharpened until it hurt to watch.

Tess did not ask how the Consortium had identified the pod of origin from an unread slip.
Halix had leaks. The Lattice had buckets.

The cradle scale rose another fraction.

The stone sat under its ringless air, quiet and complete, becoming heavier by being wanted.

## III. The Seller

Tess closed the counter shutters.

Not fully. A full shutter meant fraud, death, or an argument involving knives. She lowered
them to privacy height, which let the buyers see that they were being excluded but not yet
insulted. Halix etiquette contained many such useful half-cruelties.

The courier watched the shutters settle.

"Can you do that?"

"I own the bench until I fail to pay for it."

"Will you fail?"

"Not today."

He looked toward the gray blur of the Consortium woman beyond the shutter. "She offered
retuning."

"I heard."

"The frame is bad. My aunt said sell if viable. That is viable."

"Yes."

"Then why are you stopping?"

Tess folded the witness slip along its original crease. She needed a clean sentence. Clean
sentences were difficult when money had entered the room honestly.

"Because she is not buying the stone," Tess said. "She is buying permission to tell everyone
what the stone means."

"What if we want to sell that?"

"Then sell it."

He blinked.

Tess pushed the case, the slip, and the muted offer list toward him. "I am not your elder. I
am not Solace. I am not here to save you from an ugly bargain. You can accept the highest
bid. I will take my commission and write the seal accurately."

"But you think we should not."

"I think 'should' is an expensive word people spend with other people's hunger."

He sat with that.

Behind the shutter, someone laughed too loudly. The sound pressed against the counter and
lost confidence. Somewhere below, a docking clamp struck home and the deck remembered
for a moment that it was a ship before it remembered that it was a market.

"My aunt said," the courier began, then stopped.

"What?"

"She said if it had memory, sell high. If it had none, bring it back. But if it had none and
they still wanted it, ask you what they were buying."

Tess looked at him for a long moment.

"Your aunt is worse than expensive."

"She used to tune frames."

"That explains the cruelty."

He almost smiled. It failed. "What are they buying?"

Tess rested two fingers on the closed case. The polymer was warm from the booth, not from
the stone.

"The cabinet buyer is buying grief that has become safe to look at because it belongs to
someone else. The Consortium is buying a proof it can scale. The other brokers are buying
the chance to sell both to people with cleaner hands."

"And what would you buy?"

Tess almost answered nothing.

Then she looked at the cradle scale again. It had not fallen. The stone remained a little
heavier than matter justified.

This was not a new law. It was an old one doing old work in a smaller room. Collective
attention made weight. A station survived because enough people remembered it as home.
A name survived because enough mouths returned to it. A completed gift, dragged under
market light, gathered gravity from the crowd that wanted to use its completion as evidence.

The stone was not empty after all.

It held no memory. But it had become the center of a memory being made around it.

That was more dangerous.

"I would buy silence," Tess said.

The courier frowned. "Silence?"

"Not secrecy. Silence. The kind where a thing is allowed to stop performing."

"What is that worth?"

Tess opened her private ledger.

Her renewal bond sat in the upper corner, a number she knew too well. Bench license,
airshare, ring maintenance, two bribes she called facilitation because accuracy had limits.
If she touched the bond now, the next renewal would hurt. If the Consortium marked her
unreliable, the renewal would hurt more. A sentimental broker was a broken scale; Halix
had many ways of saying it.

She typed a number.

Not the Consortium's. She could not match retuning. Not the cabinet buyer's. She would
not pretend display and silence carried the same price. But high enough to pay for temporary
frame work, transport, and the aunt's next impossible month. High enough that she felt the
loss before she authorized it.

She turned the ledger to him.

"This is my purchase offer. No commission. Immediate transfer. Your aunt keeps the case
and the original witness slip. I keep the stone."

He stared. "You said it has no usable memory."

"It doesn't."

"Then why pay?"

"Because the market made it heavy while it was sitting on my bench. I am responsible for
some of that."

"What will you do with it?"

"Let it finish."

"It is already finished."

"Then I will stop making it start again."

He looked at the number for a long time. Tess let him. No ethical bargain improved by
being hurried. That was one of the few things Solace and Halix agreed on, though Halix
usually charged for the waiting.

"The Consortium offered more," he said.

"Yes."

"A lot more."

"Yes."

"My aunt will ask if I was cheated."

"Tell her yes. Then show her the ledger and let her decide by whom."

The courier's mouth tightened. He looked younger when angry. Better. Anger had more
weight than helplessness.

"You are strange," he said.

"You came to Halix with a dead seller's case and a sacred object. We are all doing our
best under poor conditions."

He touched the edge of the ledger, not accepting yet.

"If I say no?"

"I raise the shutters. You choose another buyer. I write the seal accurately."

"And you take commission."

"Of course."

This time the smile arrived, briefly and without pleasure. "Honest like Halix."

"That is not a compliment."

"My aunt said it was the only kind we could afford."

He pressed his thumb to the ledger.

The transfer cleared.

For one breath, nothing happened. Then the cradle scale dropped back to material truth.

Not entirely. But enough.

## IV. No Lot

Tess raised the shutters.

The cabinet buyer understood first. He looked at the courier, then at the ledger light, then
at Tess with genuine disappointment. Collectors always felt personally rejected by reality.

"Withdrawn?"

"Purchased."

"By whom?"

"The bench."

The Consortium woman did not move. "You acquired it personally."

"Yes."

"To resell?"

"No."

"That is an irrational classification."

"No. It is an unprofitable one."

"Vlin." The woman's voice softened by one trained degree. "You are mistaking completion
for privacy. They are not the same. This object could help normalize inheritance practice.
It could increase voluntary pledges. It could bring more clean stones into circulation and
reduce coercive supply."

Tess had to respect the angle. It was a good one. It might even be true, in the large
bloodless way large systems were sometimes true.

"At what scale?" she asked.

"Meaningful scale."

"There it is again."

"What?"

"Your favorite knife."

The cabinet buyer made the mistake of enjoying that. The Consortium woman did not.

"You cannot keep every completed inheritance out of commerce."

"I only have one."

"Others will come."

"Then others will have brokers."

"And if they come to you?"

Tess looked down at the stone. "Then I will be poorer."

The woman's face closed. Not anger. Filing.

"The Lattice prefers reliable counterparties."

"The Lattice prefers mirrors."

"Careful."

Tess was careful. That was the worst part. Every word now had mass. The courier's transfer
receipt sat complete in the ledger. The case lay open. The stone had no voice, but the room
had gathered around it again, hungry for the next classification.

She typed with two fingers.

Lot status: no lot.

Reason: completed carrier, no active memory, bench withdrawal.

The mesh resisted. Halix systems did not like "no lot." They liked sold, failed, counterfeit,
restricted, pledged, burned, released. "No lot" existed for objects that had entered the
ledger by mistake: a child's toy in a reliquary case, a devotional bead misfiled as civic
memory, once a tooth. Tess had used it three times in twelve years.

She sealed the entry.

The counter gave a low refusal tone.

Tess placed her left palm flat on the bench and leaned enough weight into it that the old
sensors read her personal bond. The refusal tone steadied, reconsidered, and accepted.

Her renewal number dimmed.

There. Cost, small and exact.

The Consortium woman glanced at the ledger. "You spent bond weight on a null entry."

"I spent it on an accurate entry."

"Accuracy would have noted market interest."

"Market interest is not memory."

"In your own physics, attention carries weight."

"Yes," Tess said. "That is why I am not feeding it."

The woman studied her then, perhaps for the first time not as a counterparty but as an
obstruction with a pulse.

"There will be a review."

"This is Halix. There is always a review. Sometimes there are three, if lunch is slow."

The cabinet buyer left first, irritated by the absence of drama. The Consortium woman
left after him, slower, leaving no card because cards were for hopeful negotiations. The
other inquiries died from the mesh one by one. Somewhere in the fourth deck, a broker
who had been waiting to underbid her cursed softly enough that she could pretend not to
hear.

The courier remained.

"Do I take the case?" he asked.

"And the slip."

"You need provenance."

"No."

"How will you know what it was?"

Tess closed the case around emptiness and pushed it to him with the folded slip on top.

"I know enough."

He took both. His hands shook now that the money had cleared. Bodies were often late
to their own survival.

"My aunt will ask what happened to the stone."

"Tell her your broker made a poor investment."

"She will ask the real answer after that."

Tess picked up the stone with bare fingers.

It was cool. Lighter than it had been under the market's attention. Lighter than a thing with
so much trouble around it had any right to be.

"Tell her it was allowed to stop."

The courier nodded once. Drift Clade gratitude was often indistinguishable from good
posture. He tucked the case under one arm and left without another word.

Tess watched him go until the crowd folded him in.

Then she locked her counter.

## V. Release

Halix had many exits and very few doors.

Tess took the service spiral down two decks and outward through the maintenance throat,
past the stalls that sold apology incense and the sleeping niches rented by the quarter-hour.
The ship thinned toward the skin. Markets liked centers. Hulls were honest about edges.

At the outer lock, a maintenance clerk looked up from a cracked slate. He saw Tess, saw
the closed fist, and looked away. Halix workers survived by knowing which transactions
were none of theirs.

"Release?" he asked.

"Small object."

"Declared?"

"No lot."

That earned one glance. Only one. "No lot costs."

"Already paid."

He opened the inner latch.

The release chamber was the size of a pantry and smelled of cold metal. Its walls were
scratched with names, numbers, apologies, and price marks. Tess disliked the price marks
most. Even at the edge of vacuum, someone always wanted the last word to be an estimate.

She stepped inside. The clerk sealed the door behind her.

Through the small port, the Helios Drift lay in a long dark curve. Not empty. Never empty.
Dark matter scaffolded the region with all the forgotten-but-not-erased weight the universe
had not yet surrendered. Somewhere beyond sight, clade pods clung to their frames. Farther
in, Consortium nodes pretended not to drift. Between them, Halix moved its warm yellow
body through the outer sleeve, selling what people could not carry and buying what they
could not admit they wanted.

Tess opened her hand.

The stone rested in her palm. Without the case, without the slip, without the buyers, it
looked like nothing much. A carrier. A used object. A small gray conclusion.

She tried to imagine the donor and failed. Tried to imagine the hearer and failed. That was
proper. Their names were on a slip now leaving with a courier, where names belonged. The
stone had no more testimony to give her.

"You did it," she said.

The words sounded foolish in the chamber. Tess let them remain.

She placed the stone in the release cup and closed the inner grate. The mechanism read
mass, found almost none worth noting, and asked for confirmation. She pressed her thumb
to the plate.

The outer seal opened.

A small breath of Halix air took the stone.

It did not shine. It did not sing. It moved away from the ship with the plain obedience of
matter no longer being asked to mean more than it could bear.

Tess watched until she could no longer distinguish it from dust.

When she returned to the fourth deck, her counter had received three complaint notices,
one procedural review, and a private message from the Consortium office requesting a
future conversation in language polished smooth enough to cut skin. Her renewal bond
sat wounded in the ledger. Her ring hung above the bench, catching stall light along its
lower curve.

She sat.

For several minutes she did not open the counter. She listened instead.

Halix was loud. Halix was always loud. Bargains, docking clamps, kettle steam, grief
lowered carefully into rings, laughter arriving where it should not and leaving quickly.
Under it all, the ship's own civic memory held: I am a market, I am a ship, I am a place
where value can be named.

Tess touched the tuning ring.

It gave back the good silence.

Not mercy. Not absolution. Merely a clean interval in which nothing was being appraised.

Then she opened the counter, because the next customer was already waiting, and Halix,
being Halix, had not finished needing her.
