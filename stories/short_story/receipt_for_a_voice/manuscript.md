# Receipt for a Voice

The corner outside the sleeping cells could no longer decide which wall it belonged to.

Yenu had marked the left side with chalk at first watch. At second watch the mark was still
there, but approaching from the galley put it on her right. Hol-Vren had measured both
walls. The lengths agreed; the turn between them did not. He had closed the sleeping
cells and sent twelve people to bunks in the workshop.

He stood beside Yenu now with the frame-service notice in his hand. She had seen his
face before she saw the notice.

"The filament?" she asked.

"Bought, cut, packed. Still at the inner relay."

"Then send someone to fetch it."

He held up the notice. H-37, the corridor booked for their freight from the inner
Drift, had lost its clean certification. A Fleet clerk had found an anchor
burn hidden in the charting record. The route itself still held. Pilots could no
longer be told it held for free.

"Lysa Korr," Yenu read from the notice. "That's the pilot?"

"The one who paid the missing fare. She died years ago."

H-37 would take at least twelve cycles to recertify. Their frame would not wait.
They had saved for the filament one repair at a time, putting off the galley heat
and the membrane seals. The money had purchased passage on
the clean route. The alternate cutter could carry the package sooner, but its
pilot required another tuned personal memory stone for the release end. Fleet
stocks on that line were already spoken for.

Yenu read the notice again. A clerk's signature stood beneath the suspension:
Corin Tave. She wondered how many people were cursing him just then.

"We could have had the filament before they corrected it," Hol-Vren said.

"They corrected it because it wasn't true."

"Both things can be true."

He said it without reproach. She hated that a little. She would have preferred a
bad argument she could win.

At the corner, a workshop runner tried to carry bedding through the turn. For an
instant he faced a wall where he expected the passage. He stopped, backed up, and
waited for Yenu to tell him which way the sleeping cells had been. He knew as well
as she did. The asking helped the passage hold still long enough for him to turn.

"Five cycles," Hol-Vren said when the runner was gone. "After that I close this
deck. The whole deck, if the frame keeps slipping."

"The other route?"

"Tess Vlin has the tender at Halix. She knows who is flying."

Yenu looked once more at the chalk mark. It was still on the wall. She could
reach it if she took two careful steps.

"I'll go."

Hol-Vren folded the notice. "Take the workshop accounts. She'll want to know
what we can pay."

"She already knows."

"She knows what we could pay before someone remembered Lysa Korr."

* * *

The tether cup took six hours to reach Halix. It had no memory drive, only the
long filament and the recitations of three pods that kept the line in place. Yenu
spent the ride checking the accounts anyway. The figures did not improve when
read in different orders.

Tess Vlin's booth was open, though the tuning ring hung above an empty cradle.
Her counter had always been crowded with things awaiting a price. Now it held
only two receipts and a repair invoice.

"Your grandfather's pod," Tess said when she saw Yenu.

"Mine too."

Tess accepted the correction. "Sit."

Yenu laid the accounts on the counter. Tess looked at the top figure, then turned
the sheet over to see who had signed for the frame filament. Hol-Vren's mark sat
beside the pod elder's. They had paid for the material, the cutting, and the
ordinary corridor passage.

"It's the extra stone," Tess said.

"I know."

"There's no certified one on this deck at a price your pod can manage. The
Consortium has reserves, but its conditions arrive after you agree to the
price."

"I met their conditions once. They were very polite."

Tess looked up. She had been at the bench when Aurel Ket offered to make Yenu's
grandmother's voice part of a lattice node. She had also watched Yenu take the
stone home.

"Did you release it?" Tess asked.

"After I heard her. Yes."

Tess nodded. "Even if you had kept the carrier, it would be closed. Nothing for
a drive to draw from."

"I know that too."

Yenu had not come to sell her grandmother a second time. She had come hoping Tess
knew a less obvious account to charge.

Tess brought up the alternate cutter's manifest. It was a small Canticle vessel
with room for the filament crate and little else. The assigned pilot was Elior
Ren. He had accepted the longer route on condition that the extra memory input
be assayed and named in the pre-run record. No anonymous stones. No estimate of
loss entered after release.

"He's difficult," Yenu said.

"He's flown one supervised correction. Spent a classroom smell doing it, by the
record."

"A smell?"

"He can still remember the schoolroom. He can't smell it. Ask him whether that
was a small loss."

Tess closed the manifest. She touched the repair invoice on her counter, then
seemed to wish she had not.

"I could have advanced the fare last season," she said. "I can't now."

"Because of the stone you bought."

It was a different family's closed bequest. The family had needed frame work;
buyers had offered far more for the right to exhibit or study the proof of its
completion. Tess had purchased the used carrier herself and sent it out of
Halix without its names. The market had charged her bond for making a valuable
lot disappear.

"That was my money to spend," Tess said. "This would be yours."

Yenu watched a price inquiry flash along the edge of Tess's counter. Tess put a
hand over it without reading.

"Would you spend it the same way now?" Yenu asked.

Tess took her hand away. "I did. That's why we have to answer the question in
front of us with what we still have."

Neither of them spoke for a while. Through the booth opening, Yenu could hear
the fourth-deck market doing its ordinary work. A child wanted a second helping
of fried dough. Two traders disputed a seal. Someone tested a tuning ring badly;
its note cut off as soon as it began.

"I can make a stone," Yenu said.

"You can consent to a rendering. Then I can make one. Those are separate acts."

"I understand."

"Bring me a memory that isn't part of the pod's common recitation. No names we
need to keep the walls. Something yours."

Yenu tried to think of something easy. The taste of the first bad meal she had
cooked. An afternoon repairing a shuttle latch in Hol-Vren's shop. The cup ride
to Halix when she had been little. Each had a shape but too little weight for
the release correction Tess showed her on the cutter's chart.

Then she found the memory. It had enough weight. She knew before she told Tess.

"My grandfather corrected me once," she said. "Near the end. I was reciting his
life and I said he hadn't remarried because he didn't want to. He said he
couldn't afford to."

"Your clade knows the correction."

"They know the words. I remember how he said them."

"Yenu."

"Measure it first."

Tess lowered the ring over the empty cradle. She did not reach for Yenu's hand.

"You decide after the assay," she said. "Not while you're thinking about the
corner back home."

Yenu almost smiled. Her grandfather had told her the same thing before giving
her the bequest. Tess might have remembered; she might simply have learned what
the words cost.

* * *

The ring found a clean boundary around the memory, but not a comfortable one.

Yenu stood in the booth with both palms on the cradle. The sound of her
grandfather's correction returned to her. His breath caught between the first
two words. He had been embarrassed to say them, though he had refused to let
her recitation be wrong. She had thought then that he was correcting a fact
about money. Only later had she understood that he was also letting his wife
remain in the sentence.

The ring let go. Yenu gripped the edge of the counter.

Tess read the assay without speaking. She turned the chart toward Yenu. The
memory was personal, bounded, sufficient for the cutter's extra release-end
trim. It did not include her grandmother's voice or the pod's recitation of her
grandfather. Tess could cut it cleanly enough that Yenu would retain the fact of
the conversation.

"Cleanly enough," Yenu repeated.

"That's an assessment, not a promise about how it will feel."

Tess had one question left on the consent form. If the route was canceled after
the rendering, did Yenu want the memory stone returned to her, or did she want
the unused stone destroyed? Either way, the experience would no longer be in
her mind. A returned stone could let her listen to the recording. It could
not put the lived memory back.

Yenu looked at the empty cradle. Her grandmother's stone had been made to be
heard by her. This one would be made to carry a cutter out of a corridor. She
did not want to keep it on a shelf if the flight failed to leave.

"Return it," she said. "I'll decide what to do with it then."

Tess marked the form.

The comm pane at the rear of the booth brightened with a call from the pod.
Yenu had asked her mother to wait until after the assay.

Her mother listened to the proposal with her hands folded out of view. Yenu
knew they were folded because they always were when her mother was deciding not
to reach across distance and take a choice away from her.

"I'll still know what he said," Yenu told her.

"I know."

"You remember his voice."

"Yes."

"If I get the words wrong later, correct me."

Her mother shook her head. "I will correct you if you get *him* wrong. You've
been allowed your own memory of his voice. I'm sorry this is the one you found
that fits."

Yenu had expected permission or an offer to take her place. Her mother gave her
neither. The restraint hurt. It also left the choice where it belonged.

"How's the corridor?" Yenu asked.

"Hol-Vren moved the bedding again. He has five cycles on the board."

"I'm signing."

"All right," her mother said. She kept the comm open.

Tess read the consent aloud. She named the specific experience to be rendered,
the limits of the assay, the route that would consume the stone, and the fact
that the memory could not be restored. Yenu corrected one line: the grandfather
had said *could not afford to*, not *would not*. Tess made the change and read
it back.

Yenu placed her thumb on the plate.

The extraction took less time than the assay. The ring pressed lightly against
her temples. She felt herself preparing to hear his voice, felt the small lift
of expectation before an old exchange began. Then she was looking at Tess
across the cradle, and there was a stone inside it.

She knew she had signed. She knew what the stone contained. She remembered her
grandfather's bed, the blanket, the sentence she had spoken incorrectly. When
she reached for the sound of his answer, she found only the corrected words in
her own voice.

"It's done," Tess said.

Yenu nodded. The booth had not changed. Somewhere on fourth deck, the same
traders were still disputing their seal.

Her mother was still on the comm pane.

"Yen?" she said.

The name came easily. Her grandmother's voice remained in Yenu's mind, warm in
the mid-range, calling her by that shorter name. Yenu held it for a moment.

Then she looked at the new stone and found she could not bear to hear her
grandfather on demand, even if the cradle could make a copy of its contents
before the cutter spent them.

"Send it," she said.

* * *

The outbound reliquary courier took the stone to the inner relay. Its manifest
listed a voluntary personal rendering, one donor, one specified use. The
courier paid its ordinary route tax from its own stores. Tess showed Yenu that
entry too.

On the next watch, Elior Ren accepted the stone into the cutter's drive.
His pre-run audit showed eleven responsive anchors. His route acceptance named
H-37's suspension and the longer line he would actually fly. Beside the donor
entry he wrote that a living witness at Halix had confirmed consent after the
rendering, while Yenu could still describe what she had lost.

Yenu watched the cutter's progress from Tess's counter. The notation for the
alternate corridor was less elegant than H-37's old green line. It bent twice
and paused at a release end where Elior held the path under attention long
enough for the stone to feed the drive.

The stone was consumed there. Yenu felt nothing new. She had paid when Tess
made it. The drive's expenditure did not take the memory a second time.

The cutter emerged at the Halix relay with its filament crate intact. Elior's
post-run audit counted eleven anchors. Fleet stocks showed the ordinary drive
stones spent. Yenu's donor entry showed the extra release correction, consumed
as authorized. H-37 remained suspended.

Tess read the audit and pushed the workshop accounts back across the counter.

"Get it home," she said.

"Your fee?"

"On the invoice. You can hate it when your walls are holding."

Yenu took the crate onto the tether cup. Halfway back, she tried again to
remember her grandfather's voice. Her mind supplied her mother's cadence,
then Tess's when she had read the corrected line. She stopped trying to choose
between them. Neither belonged to the moment she had given away.

Hol-Vren began the re-tuning before the cup had finished cooling. He worked
through two watches. Yenu fed filament into the frame while the pod elder
recited the rooms, and her mother carried the sleeping-cell names down the
corridor so the walls had people to hold. Hol-Vren stopped twice to correct a
connection Yenu had set too tight. He was exhausted and therefore particularly
exact.

At the end of the second watch, Yenu walked to the chalk mark.

It was on her left. She went to the galley and came back. Still on her left.
The repair had held the corner. The filament would need checking for months,
and the people in the workshop bunks could wait another night before moving
back, but the passage knew where it led.

Hol-Vren gave her the maintenance slate to sign. Its last entry listed the
filament, the labor, the corrected route, and the donor stone. There was no
field for why the corridor had been changed, so he had written that in the
margin: **H-37 certification suspended following correction to pilot Lysa
Korr's loss record.** Under it stood Yenu's name and the description she had
approved at Tess's bench.

Yenu signed. Then she carried the slate to the galley, where her mother was
making the kettle.

"Tell me about him," Yenu said.

Her mother glanced at the slate. "Which part?"

"The part where I said he didn't want to marry again."

Her mother set the kettle down. She gave the correction in her own voice and
kept talking. About the bench they had sold after the Bleaching. About the
work her father had taken to keep the pod's old frame in service. Things Yenu
knew; one thing she hadn't.

Yenu listened. The sound she had spent did not return. Her mother's voice was
different, alive in the room with her, and Yenu would have to learn it as it
was.

Along the sleeping corridor, someone lifted a bundle from the workshop and
started home. Their footsteps passed the repaired corner without stopping.
