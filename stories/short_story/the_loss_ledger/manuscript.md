# The Loss Ledger

## I. Clean Route

Corin Tave began the watch by weighing a lie that did not yet know it was a lie.

The chart lay open on the certification table, a sheet of black glass two handspans
wide and thin enough to flex when the room breathed. It showed one of the Helios
Drift's ordinary corridors: no eddy flags, no raveling scar, no recorded
zero-anchoring, no pilot hazard beyond the usual steady tax that made every Canticle
route honest in small print. The line ran green from mouth to release. Thousands
used it each cycle. Cargo tugs. Passenger ferries. Reliquary couriers. Thin-bellied
medical craft with their holds full of sedated names.

The chart was famous for being clean.

Corin did not trust clean things on a records desk.

He placed his left palm on the table's grounding plate and let the loss ledger wake
under his right hand. It was not a single book, though the Fleet still called it one.
The ledger occupied six sealed cabinets, a spine of cold metal behind the records
annex, and a live index linked to the Archivist Orders through a witness relay. The
pages used for daily work were thin ceramic slates that remembered pressure. Old
entries had bowed them in places. Not enough to count as damage. Enough that a clerk
learned where not to stack cups.

The slate displayed the route's certification history.

Route chart H-37. Helios Drift internal corridor. Last full recertification:
seventy-two cycles prior. Reliquary-assisted calibration only. No personal anchor
expenditure recorded. Pilot losses associated with route establishment: none.

Corin looked at the last line for a long time.

The annex hummed around him. Banks of chart cabinets clicked through their own small
memories. Somewhere beyond the bulkhead, traffic control spoke in soft bursts to
ships waiting for corridor mouths. The whole deck had the tired steadiness of a place
that kept civilization moving by refusing to raise its voice.

"Again," Corin said.

The table read the chart again.

Green line. Clean history. No personal anchor expenditure recorded.

This time Corin watched the release end rather than the route mouth. The chart's
green line thinned there by less than a hair. Most readers would have called it
screen jitter. A pilot might have called it old shear. A records clerk had fewer
beautiful words. Corin called it an unpaid line item.

He lowered the room lights and asked for anchor residue.

The chart resisted. Not like an argument. Like a floor that had been polished so
often it forgot where the old stain had entered the grain. The certification table
sent a narrow vibration through his wrist. The route line opened into layers:
reliquary mass, civic reinforcement, traffic repetition, pilot attention, old
corrections, newer corrections, institutional approval.

There, under the reliquary layer, something personal answered.

It was small and hard and wrong.

Corin held his attention on it until the table warmed. The trace did not bloom into
a memory. It could not. Burned anchors did not return because a clerk stared at their
ashes with professional discipline. But anchor burn left a contour in systems that
had used it. A route chart could carry that contour the way a healed bone carried
weather.

The trace sat at the release end of H-37, folded under the clean certification.

Pilot loss present.

Unlogged.

Corin lifted his hand. The room seemed to settle a fraction lower around him. Paper
would have rustled in an older age. In the annex, sealed slates clicked instead.

He requested the route's founding packet.

Three files came up at once: two reliquary manifests and one pilot report. The pilot
report was marked closed by death.

Lysa Korr, Canticle Fleet. Charting pilot. Status: deceased.

The official cause of loss was in the clean language the Fleet used when a person
had died where there was no body to be returned.

Final flight: corridor release failure during subsequent assignment. Personal loss
records complete. No outstanding ledger entries.

Corin did not open the death report yet. He opened the charting report first.

Lysa's voice came through as text, stripped of breath and fatigue by the archival
transcriber.

H-37 viable with two personal stones and standard attention binding. Release end
requires a lean against local thinning. Recommend green status after three routine
confirmation runs.

Clean. Competent. Dead ordinary.

Corin compared the timestamp to the residue.

The numbers did not agree.

The undocumented trace belonged between the second and third confirmation runs,
seven minutes before Lysa signed the green recommendation. The chart said reliquary
only. The route said a pilot had spent something.

He opened the loss ledger's active entry field and did not type.

If he logged the trace, H-37 would lose green certification before the next traffic
cycle. All derivative charts would become suspect. The Fleet would have to reroute
thousands through longer, rougher corridors until pilots and Archivists recertified
the line under honest load. Reliquary stores would be spent. Anchor taxes would rise
in small increments across ships whose crews had never heard Lysa Korr's name.

If he closed the field, nothing visible would happen.

The corridor would remain as stable as it had been that morning. Ships would pass.
Schedules would hold. The ledger would remain clean.

Only Lysa Korr would stay missing from the cost.

Corin set both palms flat on the table.

"Witness review," he said.

The annex door sealed itself with a quiet, procedural click.

## II. The Pilot Packet

The Archivist on witness duty arrived carrying tea in a cup with a cracked handle.

They were old enough that their sleeves had gone soft at the cuffs, and practical
enough to set the cup outside the table's memory field before looking at the chart.
Corin had worked beside them for six years and had never learned their birth name.
Archivists on witness duty used office titles when the work required less self.

"You called anomaly," they said.

"Anchor residue under a clean route certification."

The Archivist's face changed by not changing enough.

Corin turned the chart toward them. "H-37."

"That one carries a lot of traffic."

"Yes."

"Show me the layer."

He did. The table reopened the route, and the same hard trace appeared under the
release end. The Archivist bent close. Their shadow crossed the green line. For a
moment the line dimmed, not physically but in confidence, as if embarrassed by
attention.

"Personal," the Archivist said.

"Anchor burn?"

"Likely. Not a stone. Not civic leakage." They touched two fingers to the table and
waited for the witness relay to answer through their ring. "Whose packet?"

"Lysa Korr."

The Archivist closed their eyes.

"You knew her?"

"I knew her records. She charted clean corridors in dirty places."

In the annex, that counted as grief.

Corin opened Lysa's full pilot packet. The table arranged it in four columns: service
summary, anchor audits, route work, death record. He hated the efficiency of it. A
life became legible by losing depth.

Lysa Korr had entered the Fleet with fourteen responsive anchors. Mid-gravity human,
strong attention discipline, poor tolerance for political briefings, exceptional
release-end correction. Three mild burns over eleven active years. One moderate
burn during an evacuation corridor. No forbidden anchor exposure. No zero-anchoring.
Cleared for charting work because she could feel where a route wanted to lie.

There were no portraits in pilot packets. Portraits tempted clerks into mercy.

"Run the second confirmation," the Archivist said.

Corin called up the log.

At first the record behaved. H-37 opened in simulation: a modest corridor with
reliable memory currents and a slight release-end thinness. Lysa's attention pattern
held the line steady. Two personal memory stones warmed the model. The ship, unnamed
in the packet because the ship did not matter to the chart, passed through without
drama.

Then the release end puckered.

Not raveling. Not collapse. A modest refusal. The corridor remembered the mouth,
remembered most of the path, and forgot the last fraction of permission needed to
let a vessel out cleanly. Lysa corrected with reliquary weight. The route thickened,
then overcorrected. Corin saw the chart model cant six degrees toward a civic seam.

Lysa reduced feed. The route thinned again.

The log showed no emergency. No alarm. Just a pilot making a calculation small enough
to be buried by procedure.

Her voice entered the transcript.

Reliquary too broad. Correcting with personal anchor. Basic tier.

The next line had been cut.

Corin froze the record.

"There," he said.

The Archivist touched the gap. Their ring chimed once, low.

"Manual excision," they said.

"Authorized?"

"Not by the Orders."

The missing line sat between consent and result. That was where the loss should have
been named.

Corin advanced the log.

The corridor thickened by one clean degree. The release end opened. H-37 passed its
second confirmation and, after a third run with no visible anomaly, moved to green
status. The chart had been built on a correction that the certification did not
admit.

"Can you recover the excised line?" Corin asked.

"No." The Archivist said it without softness. "If the line held the anchor contents,
and the contents were burned, an excised record will not give them back."

"I know."

"Say it anyway."

Corin looked at the frozen chart. "Recovery would be restoration. Restoration is
impossible."

"Good."

They both stood with the impossibility for a while.

The table displayed Lysa's post-run audit. It had been filed as complete. Corin
opened the attached witness pane. The form had all the required fields: full name,
assignment, anchor count, responsive knots, loss classification, pilot contestation.
The answers looked correct until one knew where to look.

Anchor count before run: eleven.

Anchor count after run: eleven.

No new loss reported.

Corin checked Lysa's prior audit. Eleven anchors before the run. The report had
preserved arithmetic by deleting cost.

"She lied?" he asked.

The Archivist did not answer quickly.

"Maybe. Maybe someone corrected her report after she signed it. Maybe command decided
a basic-tier loss during a route certification would force delay and called the
burn a calibration artifact. Maybe she consented to that. Maybe she did not."

"That is too many maybes for a green route."

"Yes."

Corin opened the death record then, because avoiding it had become a form of
cowardice.

Lysa Korr died two cycles after H-37's certification on a different corridor, a
rough evacuation line near the outer Drift. The report was short. A raveling edge.
Passenger semi-nonphysicality. Lysa holding attention through release long enough
for the crew to eject into sealed reality-layer. Her final anchor count was listed
as nine, though the previous clean arithmetic made every later number suspect.

There was a note from a posthumous audit:

Pilot Korr's loss profile contains inconsistencies. No corrective action required;
pilot deceased and operational line closed.

No corrective action required.

Corin read the sentence twice. It had the weight of a door locked from the wrong side.

"She is dead," the Archivist said quietly. "The ledger cannot help her."

"It can stop using her inaccurately."

"That is different from help."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Corin almost answered. The annex answered first. Traffic control sent a routine
schedule pulse through the wall. Chart H-37 appeared in the queue: forty-two
departures in the next watch. The clean green line pulsed beside each manifest.

The corridor was not failing. That was the cruelty of it. A lie did not have to
break a thing immediately to be useful.

The Archivist retrieved their tea. It had gone cold.

"If you write this," they said, "the chart invalidates."

"Yes."

"If you do not write it, I will have to decide whether witness duty allows me to
write it over you."

"Does it?"

"Only if I am willing to make the Orders carry the first blame."

Corin looked up.

The Archivist's dry smile barely moved. "We are holy, Clerk Tave. Not innocent."

## III. Traffic Stability

Command sent a traffic supervisor instead of an officer with decorations.

That was how Corin knew they were afraid. Officers with decorations made problems
formal. Supervisors made them disappear into workload.

The supervisor entered the sealed annex with a slate already open and did not sit.
Her uniform had the gray shoulder band of Canticle traffic authority, no name visible
in the angle of the light. Corin preferred it that way. If he named everyone in this
room, the story would pretend names were the same thing as accountability.

"I have forty-two ships queued on H-37," she said. "Six passenger. Two medical. One
reliquary courier under Archivist seal. The rest cargo, but cargo keeps people fed
whether poets respect it or not."

"I respect cargo," Corin said.

"Then respect the chart."

The Archivist remained by the wall, witness ring dim.

Corin displayed the anchor trace again. "The chart is false."

"The chart has carried traffic for seventy-two cycles."

"On an unlogged personal burn."

"On a correction made by a pilot who knew her work."

"Then let the ledger say what her work cost."

The supervisor's jaw tightened. "If we invalidate H-37, Drift traffic loses its
cleanest internal line for at least twelve cycles. The alternate route taxes pilots
harder. Reliquary stores are already rationed because the outer sleeve has been
throwing eddies all week. You will move one hidden cost into forty visible costs and
call that honesty."

"I will call it accounting."

"Accounting does not keep passengers dense."

Corin almost laughed, but the sound had nowhere decent to go. "Neither does false
arithmetic."

The supervisor leaned over the table. The green route line passed across her sleeve.
"The route is stable."

"Because it learned a correction from a pilot we did not record."

"Learned?" Her eyes flicked toward the Archivist. "Is that your term or his?"

"Mine. Plain language."

"Then plainly: does the corridor care whether Lysa Korr has a ledger entry?"

The question opened the room.

Corin looked at the chart. H-37 did not tremble. No mouth puckered in sympathy. No
ghost reached out of the green line asking to be named. The corridor cared about
memory density, attention, reliquary tuning, repeated traffic, and the actual weight
spent to hold it. It had taken Lysa's anchor when Lysa offered it. It did not care
whether a clerk later made a correct note.

Physics had already been paid.

Civilization had stolen the receipt.

"The corridor may not care," Corin said. "The Fleet should."

"The Fleet is forty-two ships in the next watch."

"The Fleet is also the dead pilot inside that chart."

"No," the supervisor said. "She is not inside the chart. Her lost anchor is gone.
Do not make poetry into procedure."

That landed because it was true enough to bruise.

Corin let the silence hold. He had watched junior clerks ruin records by defending
the wrong sentence. A true objection deserved space even when it served a lie.

"Her anchor is gone," he said. "The chart contains the effect of its expenditure.
That effect was certified as reliquary-only. Every pilot using H-37 is being told a
false story about what the route requires."

"The false story is reducing panic."

"It is increasing ignorance."

"Ignorance can be operationally useful."

The Archivist spoke then. "So can amputation."

The supervisor looked at them. "The Orders do not get to moralize while your sealed
courier waits in my queue."

"I am not moralizing," the Archivist said. "I am naming categories."

The supervisor exhaled through her nose and turned back to Corin. "What do you want?"

The clean answer was easy: log the burn, suspend the chart, let truth do its damage.
Corin distrusted easy things on records desks.

"A provisional invalidation," he said. "Immediate amber status. No passenger traffic
until recertification. Medical traffic by captain's acceptance of increased anchor
tax. Cargo rerouted or delayed. Reliquary courier reassigned to alternate line with
Archivist signoff. Chart H-37 marked unclean until the route is flown under honest
modeling."

"That is still suspension."

"It is suspension with triage."

"Twelve cycles."

"Maybe less if command assigns enough pilots."

"Pilots are not paper."

"Neither are dead ones."

The supervisor's expression closed.

For a moment Corin could see the next year of his life with uncomfortable clarity.
Review boards. Quiet reassignment. The phrase judgment imbalance entered in his
personnel file by someone who had not stood over the chart. No one would call it
punishment. Records clerks were rarely punished. They were moved away from decisions
until their caution became institutional memory in someone else's mouth.

"There is another option," the supervisor said.

Corin waited.

"We file a restricted anomaly. No ledger entry until scheduled recertification. H-37
remains green with an internal caution flag. Pilots are advised to prepare for mild
release-end tax. Traffic holds. Korr's packet can be corrected later, after the route
is less loaded."

"Later is how the loss vanished."

"Later is how systems survive long enough to correct themselves."

Corin touched the edge of the table. It was warm where the route lay. "Would pilots
be told the caution comes from an undocumented anchor burn?"

The supervisor did not answer.

"Then we would be asking them to carry an unknown cost."

"Pilots carry unknown costs every day."

"Not because I hid one."

"You think this is about you?"

"No," Corin said. "That is why I am still here."

The supervisor's slate chimed. Traffic pressure, probably. Another ship asking why a
green route had not yet opened. Somewhere a child in a passenger cradle might be
counting deck lights. Somewhere a pilot might be touching anchor knots before a run
they had no reason to fear more than usual.

The supervisor read the update and looked tired in a way command language could not
polish.

"If you enter the burn," she said, "you will own the cascade."

Corin thought of Lysa Korr's packet: No corrective action required; pilot deceased.

"No," he said. "I will own the entry. The cascade belongs to what happened."

## IV. The Missing Line

Corin asked for one hour before entry.

The supervisor objected. The Archivist granted it. The witness seal held the annex
closed, and traffic control received a technical delay notice that told no lie and
almost no truth.

One hour was not enough to solve the route. It was enough to read Lysa Korr as more
than a discrepancy.

Corin opened the parts of her packet that clerks usually skipped: instructor notes,
contested classifications, personal addenda attached to anchor audits. The Fleet
allowed pilots three private lines per burn report. Most used fewer. Pain did not
always become articulate because a form offered space.

Lysa had used hers.

After her first mild burn:

Lost: texture of red thread between thumb and forefinger. Function intact. I can
still sew a pressure sleeve. I do not know why I learned.

After her second:

Lost: brother's laugh at the water pump. Brother remains known. Event remains known.
The laugh no longer arrives before his face.

After her third:

Lost: left-side warmth of winter cot. No operational effect. Do not downgrade me for
being exact.

Corin read that line three times.

Do not downgrade me for being exact.

The fourth burn, the moderate one, came from an evacuation corridor. Lysa had spent
the sound of her first instructor saying her name correctly after months of getting
it wrong. The report classified it as moderate because the name remained, the voice
did not, and the vocational anchor thinned but held. Lysa's addendum was only one
line:

Accuracy is not mourning, but it is the part command permits.

Corin sat back.

The annex light dimmed and brightened as the table adjusted to his attention. It had
learned him over six years: the long pauses, the left thumb braced against the palm
when a form made him angry, the way he reread sentences that hid harm in tidy grammar.
Machines did not love their operators. But in a universe where memory made gravity,
repetition still left a shape.

He wondered what shape Lysa had left in the pilots who used H-37 without knowing it.
Not gratitude. Gratitude required knowledge. Not debt. Debt required a creditor the
institution admitted. Something less noble and more useful: ease. A clean release.
A schedule held. Thousands of safe arrivals leaning, fraction by fraction, on a loss
filed as none.

He found the excision history buried under a maintenance checksum.

Not the missing line itself. The scar around it.

The edit had happened fourteen minutes after Lysa signed the third confirmation run.
The authorization came from traffic certification, not pilot command, and used an
emergency load-balancing code retired decades ago. Corin could not identify the hand
without opening records outside his clearance. He did not need the hand.

He needed the wound.

The Archivist came back to the table. "You found enough?"

"Enough to enter the loss. Not enough to name the lost anchor."

"That matters."

"I know."

"A ledger entry without contents can still preserve the fact of expenditure."

"It feels thin."

"It is thin."

Corin looked at Lysa's addendum again. Accuracy is not mourning, but it is the part
command permits.

"What if the part we can preserve is only accusation?" he asked.

The Archivist's hands, folded in their sleeves, looked suddenly old. "Then preserve
it cleanly."

He opened the active field.

The loss ledger did not glow. It did not sing. It displayed boxes with labels
invented by committees trying to keep sacrifice legible without making every
assignment a funeral.

Pilot ID.

Corin entered Lysa Korr.

Status.

Deceased.

Associated chart.

H-37.

Loss class.

Undocumented anchor burn, personal, exact tier unknown.

Anchor contents.

Unknown. Excised from source record. Not recoverable.

Operational effect.

Release-end correction incorporated into chart certification and subsequent traffic
modeling.

Required action.

Invalidate clean certification. Recertify under corrected anchor-tax assumptions.

Pilot contestation.

Unknown.

Witness.

The Archivist placed two fingers on the witness plate. Their ring sounded once.

The field asked for clerk signature.

Corin did not sign immediately.

He thought of the supervisor's forty-two ships. Of the medical craft. Of cargo that
kept people fed whether poets respected it or not. He thought of pilots on alternate
routes spending small sensory things that would never become stories. He thought of
how easily honoring one dead person could become a way to spend the living with a
cleaner conscience.

Then he thought of what suppression would ask of them instead: to keep spending
against a chart that lied about the ground beneath their feet.

He signed.

The ledger accepted the entry.

Nothing in the room changed for several breaths.

Then the route chart updated.

H-37's green line did not break. It did not dim into danger. The physical corridor,
far from the annex and indifferent to clerical courage, remained itself. But the
certification layer withdrew. Green became amber. Amber became a suspended lattice
of caution marks. Every derivative chart lit with a query. The table sent notices
into traffic control, pilot command, Archivist routing, reliquary stores, and the
quiet offices where institutional anger learned which name to use.

Outside the bulkhead, traffic control stopped speaking.

For one second the deck was silent enough that Corin heard the old cabinets behind
him flex under their accumulated losses.

Then the calls began.

## V. Amber Route

By the end of the watch, H-37 had become heavy.

Not physically enough to pull cups across a table. Not dramatically enough for anyone
to mistake recordkeeping for salvation. Its weight appeared in less merciful forms.

Passenger traffic held in berth. Cargo manifests split and rerouted. The reliquary
courier surrendered its clean slot and took an older line with three additional
anchor-audit requirements. The two medical ships received captain's waivers after
their pilots accepted increased release-end tax and additional Archivist witnesses.
One captain declined and delayed. One accepted and asked for the corrected chart.

Corin watched the request arrive.

Corrected chart, the message said. Not clean chart.

He sent it.

Traffic control developed a new rhythm around the wound. Longer pauses. More
explicit consent. Pilots requested loss histories before accepting H-37-adjacent
assignments. Reliquary stores complained in numbers. Command complained in verbs.
The supervisor returned once, read the reroute board without speaking, and left.

The Archivist remained until the formal witness period ended.

"You understand this will not make her more alive," they said.

Corin closed Lysa's packet. "Yes."

"It will not make the burn more meaningful."

He considered that. "I do not know."

The Archivist tilted their head.

"The anchor meant what it meant when she spent it," Corin said. "We do not control
that. But the institution controlled whether her loss became part of the route's
memory or only part of its convenience."

"And now?"

"Now it is burden."

The Archivist gathered their cold tea and did not drink it. "Burden is one of the
honest forms memory takes."

After they left, Corin stayed alone with the loss ledger.

He opened Lysa's entry once more to check for transcription errors. That was the
reflex the Fleet had given him and, in fairness, the reflex that had saved her from
remaining a calibration artifact. He found no mistakes. The entry looked small. The
kind of small that could bend a life around it.

There was one optional field at the bottom.

Clerk's continuity note.

Corin usually avoided that field. It was a dangerous place. Clerks who wrote too
much there turned records into petitions. Clerks who wrote nothing taught the next
reader to trust forms more than absence.

He touched the field.

Lysa Korr's undocumented anchor burn was used to certify H-37 as cleaner than it
was. The lost anchor contents remain unknown and unrecoverable. Future traffic must
not treat unknown as negligible.

He stopped there.

Then he added one more sentence, because the record required it and because he did
not know where else civilization put shame when it was still useful.

The route held because someone paid.

The ledger accepted the note.

The page bowed slightly under his palm.

Corin knew better than to make too much of that. Ceramic slates bowed under heat,
pressure, age, and the mechanical tolerances of imperfect fabrication. They also
bowed under memory when enough people agreed to carry what had been written there.
Both explanations could be true without making either one kind.

The first delayed passenger ship departed near the end of second watch on an
alternate line. Its pilot filed a pre-run acknowledgment that named the suspension,
the unknown burn, and the increased tax. The ship left forty-seven minutes late. No
one aboard would thank Lysa Korr. Most would never hear her name.

But their pilot had.

That was not justice. Corin disliked the word when applied to the dead. Justice
suggested balance, and memory did not balance. It accumulated. It pressed. It asked
the living to walk differently.

He shut down the certification table.

In the dim glass he saw his own reflection layered over the suspended route. A clerk
with tired eyes. A green line made amber. A dead pilot's name where the chart had
once offered none.

His personnel slate chimed.

Pending review, it said.

Of course.

Corin cleared the notice and performed the closing audit. Cabinets sealed. Witness
relay closed. Loss ledger indexed. H-37 suspended pending recertification. Lysa Korr,
deceased Canticle pilot, one undocumented anchor burn entered under correction.

He spoke the final line aloud because some records deserved air.

"No corrective action required was incorrect."

The annex did not answer.

It did not need to.

Outside, the Helios Drift kept moving around the new obstruction. Ships found other
paths. Pilots counted anchors with more care. Traffic complained and adapted.
Somewhere, a corridor that had never cared what clerks admitted continued to hold
because memory had already been spent into it.

Corin turned off the room lights.

In the dark, the ledger remained heavy.
