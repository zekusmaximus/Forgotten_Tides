# The Miniaturist's Name

## I. Prior Art

The patent gallery in the Mnemosyne Lattice Core made invention feel inevitable.

Yaret Ivo-Senn disliked it on sight.

The room descended through six terraces of black glass and archive fiber, each terrace
holding claims so old and heavy that the air leaned inward around them. Drive casings,
harmonizer coils, corridor regulators, civic scaffolds. Every object rested under a
transparent seal with its authors, its dates, its custodians, its authorized heirs. Nothing
had been allowed to pass into usefulness without first being named.

Yaret moved lightly among them.

That was not metaphor. She was Drift Clade, raised in low memory and low gravity, built
long-boned and careful. The Core pressed on her shoulders. It found the unreinforced spaces
in her posture and corrected them. Her travel shoes kept trying to remember the lighter
walk of home; the floor insisted on a public gait. By the third terrace her knees had begun
to ache in the old way, the way clade bodies ached when architecture had too much opinion.

She had not come to the gallery to admire anything. She had come because a courier from
Halix had sent her a quiet notice with no greeting and one line of warning:

*They have filed the little throat under their own names.*

The little throat was not its real designation. It had never had one. In the Ivo-Senn
workshop, parts were called by what the hand needed to know: throat, cup, comb, hinge,
notch. The throat was a miniature resonator folded inside compact memory drives so the
drive could hold a finer gradient without eating more casing. It was old work. Her
grandmother had taught it to her mother. Her mother had taught it to Yaret with a cracked
practice coil and a table that softened at one corner every fourth watch.

The Consortium patent called it a compression harmonizer for regulated courier-class
drive architecture.

Yaret found the object on the fourth terrace.

It lay beneath a seal no larger than a sleeping infant. The casing was elegant, too clean,
polished for exhibition, stripped of all workshop apology. Beside it, the patent text rose
in vertical lines:

MNEMOSYNE LATTICE CONSORTIUM INFRASTRUCTURE DIVISION.

COMPACT DRIVE STABILITY PACKAGE, INNER-RING COURIER USE.

PRIMARY CLAIM: MINIATURIZED MEMORY-GRADIENT RESONANCE THROAT.

There were three registered inventors. All Consortium. Two departments. One research
funding body. No clade.

Yaret put her palm against the seal.

The glass warmed, recognizing visitor pressure, and the internal projection enlarged. The
drive throat unfolded in pale light. Six nested turns. A split in the second return. One
deliberately uneven third notch.

There.

The third notch was the signature. Not a mark. A mistake preserved because removing it
made the whole assembly lie. Stable-faction engineers smoothed everything. They believed
symmetry meant trust. Drift Clade miniaturists knew better. A part that small had to be
left one honest imperfection or it began to believe it was an ideal object, and ideal
objects were too vain to work under corridor stress.

Her mother had said that while Yaret was eleven and angry and ruining expensive wire.

Do not flatter a drive. Let it know where it is allowed to be ugly.

Yaret had cut the third notch wrong on purpose every time since.

The projection rotated. A fine line of annotation passed over the notch and called it
proprietary tolerance variation.

Yaret laughed once. It was not amusement. It was breath leaving a struck place.

A docent across the terrace looked up. He was young by Core standards, dense in the chest,
dressed in the gray that meant Consortium authority pretending to be public service.

"Is there an issue with the display?" he asked.

Yaret kept her palm on the seal. "Yes."

He waited. The Core trained people to wait as if waiting were generosity.

"Your patent has a clade hand in it," she said.

The docent's expression did not change. "Many Drift Clade improvements are represented in
Consortium-regulated drive architecture. The gallery honors the infrastructure history of
the Core."

"It honors theft with good lighting."

Now his expression changed. Only a little. The gallery around them seemed to hear the word
and dislike it. The sealed objects remained still, but the air grew fractionally heavier.

"Attribution claims are not handled on the public terraces," he said.

"Where are they handled?"

"The recognition archive. Fifth inward tier. Appointment required."

Yaret lifted her hand. The glass cooled as if relieved.

"Make one," she said.

He looked at her travel badge, at the clade weighting in her bones, at the portable tool
case hanging from her shoulder. His eyes found every reason to make the appointment
difficult and every reason not to make a scene in front of polished theft.

At last he touched his cuff.

"Name?"

That was always the first violence.

Drift Clade names were not built for Core rooms. They were working structures, temporary
bridges between recent obligation and older release. Yaret had been Yaret Ivo-Senn for
twelve years because her hand had remained in that work. Before that, in childhood, she had
carried the lighter half of her mother's pod-name. Someday, if she left drive work or the
line chose to thin, Ivo-Senn would pass off her like dust from a sleeve. That was the
discipline. Carry only what the current task required.

The gallery wanted a permanent answer.

"Yaret Ivo-Senn," she said.

The docent repeated it into the cuff. The room accepted the name badly. It held for three
beats, then corrected the hyphen into a legal connector and filed her as if she had always
intended to become easy to search.

Yaret felt the correction under her tongue.

She took her hand away from the seal and went looking for the stairs.

## II. Insufficient Weight

The recognition archive was warmer than the gallery and less honest about why.

It had chairs with padded arms, water in sealed cups, low lamps arranged to suggest care.
The walls carried no displays. Records happened here out of public sight, which made the
room feel gentle until the chair read Yaret's travel badge and increased gravity by a polite
fraction to keep her from floating too lightly against the Core's standards.

She sat because arguing with furniture wasted strength.

The clerk behind the desk was neither old nor young. Core bodies aged through density more
than years. He had the compact stillness of a person who had spent too long near records
that outlived objection.

"Attribution challenge," he said.

"Lineage correction," Yaret said.

He glanced at the desk, where her file had opened. "You allege the patent draws from
unregistered Drift Clade prior art."

"No. I allege it draws from Ivo-Senn work."

"The distinction matters to you."

"It should matter to the patent."

The clerk accepted the rebuke by not reacting to it. "Do you have dated filings?"

"Halix repair receipts. Three courier service logs. Two compact-drive failures where the
throat was copied badly and bloomed under load. Workshop diagrams."

"Core filings?"

"No."

"Consortium contracts?"

"No."

"Archivist seals?"

"No."

"Registered inheritance claim?"

"It is not an inheritance stone."

"That is not what I asked."

Yaret set her tool case on the desk between them. The latch was worn down to dull metal
where four generations of hands had opened it. Inside lay three micro-coils, each smaller
than the last joint of her thumb, nested in gray cloth. One had been cut by her mother.
One by her grandmother. One by Yaret at fifteen, when she had finally learned to leave the
third notch ugly enough.

The clerk looked at them without touching.

"These are work samples," she said. "The pattern is consistent. The defect is intentional.
The patent copied it because the part fails without it."

"I do not doubt your technical assessment."

"Then correct the attribution."

He turned the desk toward her.

The patent record appeared in the air, its lines deep and bright. Yaret could feel its
weight across the desktop. It had already been cited by procurement offices, courier
licenses, training manuals, safety revisions. Each use thickened the claim. Each repetition
made it more itself.

Beside it, the clerk opened her submitted file.

It was thin. Halix receipts. A repair log from an outer sleeve courier. Workshop diagrams
with no institutional seal. A dead pod's maintenance note. A market broker's witness mark.
Truths, all of them. Light truths.

The two records sat side by side.

The patent did not need to argue. It was too heavy.

"This archive cannot remove authorship from a high-density patent on low-density witness,"
the clerk said. "It can append a disputed prior-art note pending further record weight."

"A footnote."

"A protected footnote."

"Useful to whom?"

"Future reviewers."

"Dead people have future reviewers too, in the Core?"

He folded his hands. "Ms. Ivo-Senn."

The corrected name landed again: Ms. The hyphen. The presumption that a name could be
owned by civility.

"Do not make it heavier by being polite," she said.

He paused. That, at least, reached him.

"Yaret," he said, carefully. "The archive is not a moral instrument. It is a structural
one. It preserves what enters with enough density to be preserved."

"My family did the work."

"Your family did not make a record the Core can hold."

"We were busy making drives small enough for your couriers to stop wasting memory."

"I believe you."

That was worse than refusal. Belief without correction was a chair offered beside a locked
door.

Yaret closed the tool case. The click sounded weak in the room.

"What would be heavy enough?"

The clerk looked at the file again. "A lineage registration."

She had expected it. The expectation did not soften the word.

"No."

"Then a disputed note is the available remedy."

"No."

"Those are the two clean options."

Drift Clade people distrusted clean options. Cleanliness hid the cut.

"Say the dirty one," Yaret said.

The clerk's eyes lifted.

"Register the Ivo-Senn line as a technical lineage under Core records," he said. "Bind the
work samples, witness marks, repair consequences, and your personal attestation into one
claimant body. The archive can then weigh the family line against the patent. If the line
holds, the attribution can be corrected without voiding the patent."

"And afterward?"

"The name becomes durable."

"Durable."

"Searchable. Citable. Legally continuous. It can receive licensing allocations, liability,
procurement exclusions, summons, settlement offers."

"Hooks."

He did not deny it.

"It can also receive credit," he said.

Credit. In the Core, the word had gravity. On a clade pod, it would have been too heavy to
bring into the galley without warning.

Yaret looked down at her hands. Long fingers. Wire scars. A burn line along the left
knuckle from a drive throat that had bloomed during testing and tried to become larger than
the bench. The scar was almost gone. Clade skin let small injuries leave without ceremony.
Not everything needed to stay.

"Can it be released later?" she asked.

"A lineage can be deprecated."

"That is not release."

"No."

"Can it be thinned?"

The clerk considered lying. She watched him decide not to.

"Not in Core systems."

There it was. A simple sentence with a large door behind it.

If she registered the name, Ivo-Senn would no longer be only a working bridge. It would
become infrastructure. It would persist after the living line wanted to put it down. It
would answer searches. It would stabilize the old workshop in public memory and make the
children who learned at its bench inherit more than their hands could choose.

It would also keep the Consortium from calling their work a proprietary tolerance.

The clerk touched the desk. "There is a third route. You may register yourself as the
named inventor of the disputed feature. Living claimants are easier to weight than diffuse
lineages. You would receive recognition and settlement."

Yaret stared at him.

"I was not born when my grandmother made the first throat."

"Many patents compress contribution histories."

"That is a beautiful sentence for theft."

His mouth tightened. "It is an available sentence."

"Not to me."

She stood too quickly. The chair's gravity caught at her hips. For one humiliating second
she had to press both palms on the desk to stay upright.

The clerk did not offer help. He was decent enough for that.

"I need one shift," she said.

"The patent will remain."

Of course it would. The Core was very good at remaining.

## III. The Reply

Yaret spent the shift in a public rest alcove where the Core softened itself for travelers.

Soft was relative. The wall gave under her back by one regulated centimeter. The air had
been tuned to mid-gravity comfort and smelled of filtered citrus, which told her the Core
believed discomfort was an error in environment rather than a useful form of knowledge.

She opened the private channel to the pod twice and closed it twice.

The third time, she sent only the patent projection and one sentence.

*They took the throat. Correction requires lineage registration.*

The reply could not come quickly. Drift Clade communications were frayed by distance,
market relays, old debts, and the habit of not making every thought immediate. Yaret set
the tool case on her lap and opened it.

The three coils lay in their cloth.

Her grandmother's was the least beautiful. The cut lines were irregular. The casing had a
small dent where an apprentice had dropped it and then learned, by terror, that a part can
survive indignity if it has been told the truth about itself. Her mother's coil was cleaner,
but the third notch remained stubborn and asymmetric. Yaret's was smallest. She had been
proud of that once. Now it looked like a child trying to win an argument with scale.

No breakthrough lived in the case. Nothing the galaxy had not already used. That was the
insult. The work had succeeded so well that it had become background. Couriers ran lighter
because clade hands had made drives less hungry. Lattice offices filed schedules as if
their messages moved through space by administrative virtue. Inner-ring engineers gave
lectures on regulation packages and never once asked why the little throat did not bloom.

Yaret touched the old coil.

Memory gathered through use. Even an object that stored nothing deliberately could grow
heavy with handling. The coil had crossed enough palms to know it was not alone. Under her
finger, it answered with a faint warmth.

Not a memory stone. Not a reliquary. Just work, remembered by hands.

Her badge chimed near the end of the shift.

The pod reply arrived as a voice packet. No image. Drift Clade elders avoided image when
the room receiving it belonged to another power.

The voice was her mother's, thinned by relay and restraint.

"We have heard. The old bench heard before we did. Its left leg stopped drifting for nearly
a minute, which annoyed the cup on it. So the Core has already put a hand on us."

Silence. A breath. Yaret held the coil until its edge pressed a mark into her thumb.

"Do not register us because you are angry," her mother said. "Anger is too light for
permanence. Do not register us because they deserve shame. Shame belongs to the one who
can feel it, and institutions are poor vessels for that."

Another pause. Somewhere behind the voice, metal knocked softly. The old workshop,
probably. Or the galley pipe that remembered water only when insulted.

"Register us only if the apprentices need the name to keep the work from being taken again.
If you do it, do not pretend it is clean. A fixed name is a nail. It can hang a tool. It can
also fasten a hand to a wall."

Yaret closed her eyes.

Her mother's voice softened.

"If you choose it, choose it as yourself. Not as our apology. Not as our rescue. We taught
you the hand. We did not teach you obedience."

The packet ended.

Yaret played it once more. Then she deleted the local copy before the Core could decide her
mother's caution was useful metadata.

For a while she sat in the alcove with the tool case open and her name waiting on every
system around her.

At home, a name could be tried on, carried, amended, released. In the Core, a name was a
load-bearing beam. The beam held. It also blocked doors.

She thought of the apprentices. Some of them not yet born. Some of them already in
workshops where Consortium parts arrived labeled as licensed originals, expensive enough
that clade repairs became infringement if the wrong person looked too closely. A name could
be a nail. It could also be a wedge in a closing seam.

Yaret closed the case.

The rest alcove asked if she required gravity assistance to stand.

"No," she said.

The wall recorded the refusal. The Core liked refusals. They made good evidence later.

## IV. Heavy Record

The registration room was below the recognition archive.

Everything important in the Core seemed to be below something else. Weight was a form of
rank. Yaret descended in a lift that named each tier in a calm voice and ignored the way
her ears tightened. By the time the doors opened, her tool case felt as if it had filled
with water.

The clerk was waiting at a long table of dark fiber. Not the same desk. Not the same room.
This room did not pretend comfort. Its walls were lined with inactive registry plates, each
one dull until called. The table had no chairs.

"You returned," he said.

"You sound surprised."

"I am not. I am acknowledging choice."

That was almost kind. Yaret disliked needing kindness from him.

He placed a blank plate on the table. It was the size of both her hands, thin as a page,
black at the edges. The center held no reflection.

"Lineage registrations require a living bearer," he said. "You may withdraw until the
first oath acceptance. After that, correction requires review."

"Correction. Not release."

"Correct."

"Say what the record will do."

He looked at her for a moment, then nodded. "It will bind Ivo-Senn as a durable technical
lineage in the Mnemosyne Lattice Core. It will attach the work samples and witness chain to
the compact drive patent. It will make the name available for attribution, licensing,
audit, summons, procurement screening, and future disputes. It will not make the Consortium
your owner. It will make the Consortium able to find you."

"And my family."

"And anyone who later claims the line."

"No one claims a clade line forever."

"They will now, if they use this one."

The sentence entered the room and stayed.

Yaret opened the tool case. She set the three coils on the blank plate in order of age.
Grandmother. Mother. Herself. No names beyond the line. No private dead for the Core to
turn into handles.

The clerk's brow shifted. "The registry will ask for individual contributors."

"It can ask."

"A refusal may weaken the claim."

"Then let it show that the family refused to trade the dead for weight."

He entered something into the side panel. "Noted."

The registry plate woke under the coils.

At first there was only a pressure change. Then the room grew warmer around the table. The
coils projected themselves in fine light, magnified and exact: three imperfect throats,
each small enough to disappear under a careless thumb. The plate read their cuts. It found
the shared asymmetry. It found the repeated scar where the return wire had to be bent
against elegance. It found the family hand.

Then it looked for a family and found air.

The projection trembled.

"State the lineage name as it must be held," the clerk said.

Yaret's mouth dried.

In clade speech, Ivo-Senn was two obligations leaning together. Ivo, the bench hand.
Senn, the line that had left stable habitats three generations back and learned how to
make parts behave in rooms that forgot corners. Joined, they meant the people currently
answerable for this work. Not blood exactly. Not property. A promise with an exit.

The Core was waiting to remove the exit.

"Ivo-Senn," she said.

The plate brightened.

"State bearer."

"Yaret Ivo-Senn."

"State authority."

"Living miniaturist. Taught in the line. Carrying the hand."

The clerk watched the plate. "State permanence consent."

Yaret looked at him.

He did not apologize. Perhaps he knew there was no clean apology for making the wound
procedural.

"The record requires it," he said.

"Of course it does."

She placed both palms on the plate beside the coils.

Cold moved into her hands. The plate was reading not her thoughts but the memory pressure
around them: the bench, the coil, the hand over hers correcting the notch, the old lesson
that a small part must be allowed one honest flaw. Personal memory. Low mass. Stable.
Enough to carry shape, not enough to overpower. She did not give the Core her mother's
voice. She did not give it the pod's galley or the names of dead apprentices. She gave it
only the work as work had touched her.

The plate accepted the boundary.

That surprised her.

The clerk saw the surprise. "The archive is hungry," he said. "It is not omnipotent."

Yaret almost liked him then, which was inconvenient.

"State permanence consent," he repeated.

She thought of her mother's sentence. A fixed name is a nail.

She thought of future apprentices paying licensing fees to repair their own inheritance.
She thought of the patent calling the old notch proprietary tolerance variation until the
words became truer than the hands that had cut it.

She thought of release.

Then she said, "I consent to durable recognition of the Ivo-Senn miniaturist line for this
work."

The room grew heavy.

Not dramatically. No alarms. No shining. The registry plates along the walls woke one by
one in a low sequence, each accepting a duplicate. Yaret's hands pressed harder against the
table. The coils did not move, but their projections deepened, lines becoming grooves the
room could lean on.

The name entered the plate.

Ivo-Senn.

For one instant it was only text.

Then it became a thing.

Yaret felt it in her wrists first, a small downward pull. Then behind the tongue, where the
name had always been light enough to alter. The hyphen, which had once been a bridge, set
like cooled metal. Not painful. Worse: accurate.

The plate weighed the lineage against the patent.

The patent resisted. Institutional memory had muscle. Procurement citations, training
manuals, departmental pride, three registered inventors, all the ordinary repetitions by
which theft becomes infrastructure. The room hummed with it.

The old coil answered.

Not loudly. It was not a reliquary. It did not sing. It simply held its shape under
attention. The mother's coil held beside it. Yaret's coil held beside both. Three small
objects, cut wrong in the same exact way, refused to be separate accidents.

The registry plate sank a fraction into the table.

The patent line changed.

PRIMARY CLAIM: MINIATURIZED MEMORY-GRADIENT RESONANCE THROAT.

DERIVED FROM: IVO-SENN DRIFT CLADE MINIATURIST LINE, PRIOR ART.

CONSORTIUM PACKAGE: REGULATION, CASING, INNER-RING CERTIFICATION.

The three registered inventors remained. Their work remained. The throat no longer
belonged to them.

Yaret exhaled.

The room did not lighten.

That was the part she had known and still hoped against. Corrected records did not weigh
less. They weighed differently.

The clerk removed the coils from the plate and returned them to her cloth with more care
than she expected.

"Attribution corrected," he said.

"Settlement?"

"A claim office will contact you."

"No."

"They will still contact you."

"Of course."

He closed the plate. The name remained faintly visible under the surface, as if the fiber
had bruised around it.

"You understand," he said, "that this correction will strengthen Consortium use of the
package as well. Proper attribution makes the patent harder to challenge on theft grounds."

Yaret laughed again. Quietly this time.

"You people can make a knife out of a bandage."

"Yes," he said.

It was the first answer from him she wholly trusted.

## V. Corrected Use

The public gallery updated before Yaret reached the stairs.

She had not meant to return there. Her feet did it, or the Core did, routing her through
the fourth terrace with the patience of a place that wanted witnesses for its own
corrections.

The compact drive throat still lay under glass.

The display text had changed.

The line was smaller than the Consortium names. Of course it was. The Core loved hierarchy
as much as it loved preservation. But the line was there, fixed into the exhibit's
provenance:

Ivo-Senn Drift Clade miniaturist line, prior art basis.

Yaret stood in front of it until two visitors drifted close enough to read what she read.
One was a student with a Core accent. The other wore courier gray. The student looked at
the throat, then the line, then at Yaret's travel badge. The badge had already updated.

YARET IVO-SENN.

No travel status. No temporary holder. No current-use name.

The student recognized her.

Recognition crossed his face with no intimacy in it. He had learned her from the room. A
minute ago she had been a stranger. Now the gallery had introduced her as a record, and his
mind had obeyed.

Yaret looked away first.

The courier leaned toward the display. "Drift Clade prior art," he murmured. "I didn't
know."

"Most people didn't," the student said.

Yaret wanted to say, *That is the point.*

She wanted to say, *Now you know too much in the wrong way.*

She said nothing.

The display glass warmed under the attention of three viewers. The line held. Her wrist
ached. Somewhere very far away, perhaps only in her imagination and perhaps not, a workshop
bench in a low-gravity pod received a little more weight than it had asked for.

The docent from the previous shift appeared at the edge of the terrace. He recognized her
now too. Properly. Officially. With the small caution given to people whose names had
acquired institutional density.

"Ms. Ivo-Senn," he said.

"No."

He stopped.

The word had come out sharper than she intended. She steadied it.

"Yaret is enough."

He nodded, but the room did not. The room had learned the formal version and preferred it.

"The correction is visible," he said. "I am glad the process resolved."

Resolved. A Core word. A word for knots tightened until they stopped looking like knots.

"It has not resolved," she said. "It has acquired a label."

He had no training for that. He gave a small professional bow and left.

Yaret remained until the student and courier moved on. Then she touched the glass again.

The throat unfolded in projection. The third notch turned through the light, no less ugly
for being public. Under it, the annotation had changed.

INTENTIONAL ASYMMETRY, IVO-SENN HAND.

There was justice in seeing that.

The justice did not purify anything. It did not return the uncredited years. It did not
make the patent less useful to the Consortium's centralizing work. It did not keep claim
offices from finding the pod or contractors from offering licensed correction services to
people who had repaired these parts before the Core learned to pronounce their names.

But the notch was no longer called proprietary.

Yaret let herself stand with that much.

Only that much.

## VI. The Name's Weight

At the outer transit hall, the Core tried to be kind again.

Travelers from lighter regions moved through a gravity taper, each lane adjusting pressure
by origin and destination. Yaret entered the Drift Clade lane and waited for her body to
remember its old ease. The floor softened by increments. Her shoulders lifted. Her knees
stopped complaining.

Her name did not lighten.

It sat in the badge, in the travel record, in the patent gallery, in the recognition plate
below the archive. It sat behind her tongue with its new cooled-metal bridge. When the
lane asked for passenger identity, she said, "Yaret."

The gate displayed:

YARET IVO-SENN.

"Yaret," she repeated.

The gate waited, courteous and immovable.

Yaret Ivo-Senn, then.

It opened.

She disliked how easy the admission became once she gave the Core what it had already
decided she was.

Her pod's second reply arrived while she waited for the outbound shuttle. This one was
text, brief enough to survive poor relays.

*The old bench holds level. Children are touching the left leg and saying the line. We have
covered it with cloth until the adults decide whether to be grateful.*

Yaret read the message three times.

A second line arrived after a delay.

*Your mother says the notch was always ugly. She says to make sure they spell ugly
correctly.*

Yaret laughed. This time it nearly became laughter.

Then the ache followed.

Children saying the line. The bench holding level. The Core's heavy record traveling
outward through market relays, courier training updates, procurement corrections, public
curiosity. A family name becoming a small gravity well in a culture that survived by not
letting wells grow too deep.

The apprentices would benefit. They would also inherit a hook.

The old workshop would firm. It would also become easier to find.

The Ivo-Senn hand would not vanish into Consortium patent language. It would also be less
able to vanish at all.

Recognition was not the opposite of erasure. It was another pressure placed on the same
bone.

The shuttle docked with a clean Core chime. Around Yaret, travelers rose and gathered
their bags. Her tool case sat at her feet. It felt heavier than it had when she arrived.
The coils inside were unchanged. Grandmother. Mother. Herself. Three small throats in gray
cloth.

Yaret picked up the case.

At the gate, the attendant glanced at her badge, then at her face. Recognition sparked,
official and mild.

"Safe transit, Ms. Ivo-Senn."

Yaret almost corrected him.

Instead she paused.

The correction would not release the name. Not here. Not anymore. It would only make the
attendant apologize for a weight he had not built and could not lift.

"Safe transit," she said.

She stepped into the shuttle.

The cabin was narrow, built for Core passengers who trusted mass. Yaret took a seat by the
small window and set the tool case across her lap. As the clamps withdrew, the Core filled
the glass: terraces, archive spines, transit rings, public corridors bright with the dense
confidence of a place remembered by too many systems to fail honestly.

Somewhere inside it, her name was cooling into permanence.

She rested one hand on the case.

"Ivo-Senn," she said quietly, testing the weight.

The name answered. Not with comfort. Not with accusation. With presence.

At home, perhaps, they would learn a new ritual for it. Not a release. The Core had taken
that option. Something smaller and more difficult: a way to carry the nail without
mistaking it for the hand, a way to hang tools on it without letting it fasten the living
to the wall.

Yaret did not know the ritual yet.

She knew only the work.

The shuttle turned toward the outer relays. The Core slid behind them, still bright, still
heavy. Ahead, the Drift waited with its lighter rooms, its soft corners, its benches that
remembered just enough to hold and forgot just enough to let go.

Yaret carried the corrected patent in the public record and the contaminated name in her
mouth.

Justice had mass.

So did a wound.

She held both, because the part was small, the notch was ugly, and the drive would fail if
anyone smoothed it clean.
