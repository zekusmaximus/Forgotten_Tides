# The Light Inheritance

## I. The Recitation

Yenu Skather-Ait counted four of her grandfather's breaths before she trusted herself to begin. In the green light of his sleeping-cell, she recited him to himself.

"You were born on the spine," she said. "Before the uncoupling. You were the third child and the second son. Your mother was Ait-Vren. Your father was Skather-Hol. You learned drive-work at eleven, miniature work at fourteen, and corridor-work at nineteen, which was already late by lattice standards but which the clade considered prudent."

Her grandfather's eyes were closed. His hands lay on the blanket like two folded letters someone had decided not to send. He was eighty-four standard and at the edge of his last raveling. The pod's medic had said one day. Maybe two.

"You worked the corridor between Halix and the lattice for eleven years," Yenu said. "You were good. You were not famous. You miniaturized the third-stage flux throttle that the lattice still uses in their inner-ring couriers, although they do not call it by your name. You came home when your back gave out. You taught miniaturization to twenty-six apprentices over thirty-one years. Sixteen of them are still working. Four of them are dead. Six of them have stopped."

She paused. *Stopped* was the Drift Clade word for a chosen thinning, when a person let their coherence disperse before an ordinary death. Six apprentices had made that choice. Her grandfather had not. The medic had named his ending plainly.

"You married Ait-Resh in your twenty-eighth year," Yenu went on, more slowly now. "She was a stone-tuner. She trained at Solace for nine years before coming back to the pod. She made the first-watch reliquary that the pod still uses for funerals. She died during the Bleaching, when so many stones soured at once that the markets crashed and three of her own works went with them. The pod could not afford to replace her bench. You did not remarry. You did not want to."

His mouth moved. A correction. She bent close.

"Not did not want to," he said. His voice was thin as paper held to a light. "Could not afford to."

She nodded, took the correction, and folded it into the recitation. Drift Clade recitation was not memorization. It was *negotiation*. The dying corrected the living, and the living carried the correction forward, and the dead remained accurate by the only means a fluid-identity people had ever trusted: by being spoken, often, by someone who had loved them recently.

"Could not afford to," she repeated. "You had three children. My mother was the youngest. She was born on the pod and has never left the pod and will not leave the pod, which the pod elder considers a virtue and you consider a small grief you do not speak about."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. A pleasure at being read correctly.

"You have one grandchild," Yenu said, and stopped, because she was the grandchild and the recitation always faltered there, the way a person reading aloud falters at their own name.

He opened his eyes. They were very pale, the pale of long low-gravity life, and they were entirely present.

"Yenu Skather-Ait," he said, finishing it for her. "Sixteen standard. Apprenticed too early because her clade apprentices too early. A runner for Hol-Vren's miniaturization shop because there was no other place to put her. Not yet a tuner. Not yet a drive-hand. *Listening* well, which is the part that matters first."

She lowered her head so he would not see her eyes do the small wet thing they were doing.

"There is one more correction," he said. "It is not for the recitation. It is for the inheritance. Reach under the bedframe."

She reached. Her hand met a small wooden case the size of a child's fist, fitted with a felt-lined latch the way reliquary cases were fitted, the way her grandmother's bench had taught her grandfather to fit them on the long evenings before the Bleaching. She drew it out.

"It is yours now," he said. "It has been yours since you were born. I held it for you because a stone wills better than a will does."

She did not open it. She knew, by the weight of it in her palm, by the small honest pressure of it against the bones of her wrist, what was inside.

"Grandfather," she said.

"Have a tuner sound it first," he said. "An assay, not your own listening. Decide when you know what's there. Not now. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Good." He closed his eyes again. "Now finish the recitation. I am tired. You were almost at the part about the chair."

She finished the recitation. She talked about the chair, which had been Ait-Resh's tuning chair and which he had refused to throw out, even after the bench was sold to pay the funeral. She talked about the apprentices. She talked about the third-stage flux throttle and what he called its *honest miniaturization*, which had been a small-hearted, careful piece of engineering and the work of which he was most quietly proud.

When she finished, he was asleep.

She sat there a long time with the wooden case in her lap before she carried it out of the sleeping-cell and into the rest of her life.

## II. The Pod Frame

The pod's main corridor was running its low-cycle hum. On Skather-Ait they did not bother with day-night cycles the way lattice stations did; they ran on watch-cycles, four hours apiece, soft and rhythmic, so a body would feel always slightly drowsy and slightly attentive at the same time. It was, the pod elder said, the natural cadence of people who lived inside something they could not entirely trust to remain solid.

The walls were murmuring tonight.

Yenu noticed it three steps into the corridor — a small, soft *forgetting* in a corner near the membrane-seam, the way a piece of cloth might forget for an instant that it was woven, become for half a second only loose strands, and then remember itself. She had grown up with the pod's small forgettings. She had never grown used to them. Tonight there was a new one near the sleeping-cells, and the old one near the galley felt deeper than yesterday.

The coherence-frame was failing.

Skather-Ait was held together by two things: its physical hull, which was simple, and its coherence-frame, which was complicated. Their frame was a low-resonant mesh of tuned filaments through which the pod's collective recitations were threaded — the names, the dailies, the lullabies, the genealogies — so that the pod *remembered itself as a thing that held*. Without it, this hull was just metal and polymer, and metal and polymer alone, in their sleeve of the Helios Drift, was not enough.

Their frame needed re-tuning every eight or nine cycles. The pod's last re-tuning had been thirteen cycles ago. Hol-Vren had been stretching its service life for two seasons with calculations and temporary braces.

He was at the new soft place near the sleeping-cells, holding a measuring cord against the wall. The corner had bowed a finger's width beyond the wax mark he had made yesterday.

"I moved the two youngest into the galley," he told Yenu. "I'll brace this at fourth watch. The filament still needs replacing."

"Can I help?"

"When you come back." He rolled up the cord. "Go see the elder."

She did not touch the wall. Touching a softening wall, Hol-Vren had taught her at eleven, only taught the wall to forget your hand was a hand.

The pod elder was in the galley, doing the kettle. On Skather-Ait, he always did the kettle on third watch. It too was a recitation: *I am still here, the kettle is still hot, water still knows how to be water at this temperature.*

"Sit," he said, without turning.

She sat. She put the wooden case on the table between them, latched.

He poured her a cup of the bitter green that the clade drank for company. He poured himself one. He sat down across from her and looked, very deliberately, at the case, and then, very deliberately, away from it.

"He gave it to you," he said.

"Yes."

"Have you listened?"

"No."

"Good." He drank. "Listen at Halix. Tess Vlin's bench. She is not honest the way Solace is honest, but she is honest the way Halix is honest, which is good enough for a valuation. Do not let her put it on the open shelf. Tell her you are inheriting it. She will know what that means."

"Elder," Yenu said, because she had been raised right and the question had to be asked outright before it could be answered sideways, "are you telling me to sell it?"

He set his cup down. He looked at her with the small, terrible kindness of a man who had been a pod elder for thirty-one years and had said this kind of sentence too many times.

"I am telling you where the bench is," he said. "Hol-Vren estimates the filament and retuning together will cost eight cycles of the pod's pooled wages. A stone like the one in that case might be worth twelve cycles' wages, possibly fourteen, depending on the assay. Your grandfather knew the math. He guarded that stone since before you were born. He told me only where to send you."

She looked at the case.

"He told me to promise to decide later," she said.

The elder nodded once.

"Then decide later," he said. "But decide at Halix, not here. You cannot decide here. Here the walls are doing the deciding."

The kettle did its small final hiss. He poured her a second cup she did not want.

"Your mother is in the membrane-room," he added, "doing the recitation for your grandfather. She will be there until fourth watch. Go before she comes out. If you say goodbye to her she will say something she will be sorry for later, and you do not need that on the shuttle."

"Elder."

"Go."

She went.

## III. The Tether-Shuttle

The tether-shuttle to Halix was a six-hour ride on a thin filament the pod shared with two other Drift Clade pods and one Remembrancer outstation. The shuttle was a cup of pressurized air on a hook, basically; it had no Memory Drive, because this tether did not need one. The filament's shared recitation was enough to hold this line.

She sat in the cup with the wooden case in her lap and her knees drawn up against the cold, and she did not open the case, because she had promised, and because she was afraid.

She had never traveled with something that could pay for everyone else's shelter. Each time the cup swayed, she put a hand over the latched case.

She knew the stone held her grandmother's voice. She did not know what that voice would say. The Bleaching had taken her grandmother four years before Yenu was born, and her grandfather had held the stone through all sixteen years of Yenu's life.

The filament hummed. The cup swayed. Somewhere out the small porthole, the Helios Drift turned its slow blue weight, and an unremembered star did its work at the center of nothing.

Halix Exchange appeared as a smear of warm yellow light at the end of the third hour, swelled into a smear of warm yellow shapes at the end of the fourth, and resolved at the end of the sixth into what it was: a bazaar-ship four kilometers long, hung with banners in seven languages, smelling — even through the cup's filters — of incense, hot oil, sweat, ozone, and the faint cold metal smell of reliquary work being done at scale.

The cup docked. The clamps engaged. Yenu unfolded herself out of it and into the noise.

It hit her like a held breath being released into her face. Cries, bargains, recitations, prayers, the dense low hum of tuning rings working in a dozen booths at once. The air was *warm* — wrongly warm, the way a body is warm — and that was the first thing the clade in her noticed and disliked. A bazaar was a body. Bodies wanted things. She had been raised to be suspicious of any place that wanted things on its own behalf.

She walked. She had never been to Halix and she had been told the layout by three different cousins, none of whom had agreed, so she walked and let her ears find the bench.

She found it by sound.

Tess Vlin's counter was on the fourth deck, in the quieter quarter where the inheritance work was done, and Yenu knew it was the right counter because three booths before she reached it she heard, very faintly, the *good silence* a tuning ring made when it was lowered properly over a stone. A ring being lowered well had a sound like a held breath. A ring being lowered badly had a sound like a held breath someone meant to charge you for.

Tess Vlin's ring sang held.

Yenu stopped at the counter and waited. The brokerwoman was finishing with a customer — a heavy-set lattice man in a good coat, who was being told, politely and at length, that his stone was a fake. He took it badly. He left. Tess Vlin did not watch him go.

She looked up at Yenu instead.

She was a small woman, middle-aged, mid-gravity by build, with the long forearms of a tuner and the careful neutral face of a broker, and her eyes did the thing eyes did in this trade: they took Yenu in once, fully, and filed her under *Drift Clade, young, inheriting, alone, scared, will not be cheated by accident.*

"Sit," Tess said. "Show me."

Yenu sat. She placed the case on the bench. She unlatched it. She did not lift the stone.

Tess looked at the stone in the case for a long moment without touching it. Her face did not change. Then she said, quietly:

"That is a light-bequest."

"Yes."

"From a grandparent."

"Both."

Tess's eyebrows lifted, the smallest fraction. "Both? The donor and —"

"My grandmother tuned it. My grandfather held it for me. Since before I was born."

"Then it is a *missed-generation* light-bequest. Long-held, too. The hold itself has thickened it." She set her hands flat on the bench. "Child. Before I lower the ring. You understand what you have brought me."

"I understand."

"Tell me, then."

It was a test, of course. Halix benches did not work with people who did not know what they were selling. Yenu sat up straighter. Her clade voice came out a little formal, the way it did when she was reciting.

"A light-bequest," she said, "is a memory stone left for a later family hearer. This one is the missed-generation kind. My grandmother and I never met. She recorded a memory for someone who did not yet exist; my grandfather held it; now I inherit it. My grandfather said this stone was tuned in two registers. The first is the donor's, a personal memory and low-mass. The second is the *absence of the hearer* — the place where the recitation was meant to land and could not. Together they make this stone behave," — she searched for the word — "*inhabited*. Not civic. Not quite personal. My grandfather taught me it would be unstable in a drive and dangerous to burn. We would honor this stone by hearing or releasing it, not spending it."

Tess looked at her for a long second. Then she said, with the small involuntary respect of a professional acknowledging another professional, "Who taught you that."

"My grandfather. Two days ago. He was already dying. He thought I should know what was coming."

"He was a good teacher."

"He was."

"All right," Tess said. "The ring will give us a trace. Keep your hands off the stone. It was tuned for your listening, and I don't know what will happen when you take that listening for yourself. I will not put it on the shelf or name a price until you ask me to. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"And, child." Tess paused with her hand on the ring. "If you start crying, do it quietly. People say the booth two down is a Synod front. I can't prove it. Its buyers listen for grief the way other people listen for tells."

"I won't cry."

"I have heard that before."

She lowered the ring.

## IV. The Two Registers

The booth thickened.

Yenu had heard tuning before — at the pod funerals, at the elder's bench, once at a Solace outstation when she was nine — but she had never heard a stone tuned that had been made for her. The difference was the difference between hearing a name spoken in a room and hearing your own name spoken in a room. The air changed shape around it.

The first register came up first: a woman's voice, mid-range, warm, calling a name. The name was *Yen* — not Yenu, *Yen*, the family diminutive her mother used for her. Before the Bleaching, her mother had told the grandmother what she hoped to call a daughter. The voice said *Yen* three times, and beneath it came the smell of pod-air through a cooling vent and the papery rustle of someone's sleeve. Then the voice thinned, and Yenu reached toward the case before she remembered Tess's warning.

Yenu had not cried at her grandfather's bedside and she did not cry now. She had been told not to and she had been raised to obey small useful instructions.

But the second register rose under the first, the way Tess had warned her it would, and the second register was the one that did the damage.

It was not a voice. It was the *shape of a listening that had never happened.* It was the cup-shape her grandmother had tuned into the stone for a granddaughter she would never meet — the carved hollow where Yenu's young attention was supposed to fit, and had not, because Yenu had not existed yet. Now she sat at a Halix counter, hearing the place that had been waiting for her.

The hollow was the size and shape of her. She kept her hands beneath the counter and let the ring show her its edge. She could hear the empty place without stepping into it.

Tess Vlin lifted the ring at the end of forty seconds, very gently, the way a person closes the lid of a piano that someone else has been playing.

The booth thinned again.

Yenu sat with her hands in her lap and her face entirely composed and she did not cry, because she had said she would not, and because she had no wish to test Tess's warning about the booth two down.

"The donor knew what she was doing," Tess said. "The holder kept it well. I haven't heard one this clean in nine years."

"What would someone pay?" Yenu asked.

"This is a valuation, not money on the counter." Tess named a range. "I would need a buyer."

The low end was twelve cycles of the pod's pooled wages; the high end, fourteen. Eight would pay for the filament and Hol-Vren's work. The two children could move back to their sleeping-cell. Her mother could stop shifting the same debt from one ledger to the next.

Yenu sat with the number in her ears for a moment.

"All right," she said. "Thank you. I would like to —"

"Wait," said another voice.

She had not heard the second person come up to the counter. He had, she realized, been waiting one booth over, in line behind a fictional customer, doing the slow patient circling that lattice fixers did when they wanted to look like they had only happened by.

He was lattice. Mnemosyne, by the cut of the coat — that careful gray that meant Consortium money without ever quite saying so. He was not unkind-looking. People in his line of work seldom were; an unkind face cost the Consortium too much in negotiations.

"Forgive me," he said. "I could not help overhearing. May I make a counter-offer?"

Tess Vlin's face did the small closed thing professional faces did when they were about to be polite to someone they would have preferred to throw off the deck.

"This is an inheritance valuation," she said. "Not an open sale."

"Of course," he said. "I am not bidding to the bench. I am bidding to the child."

He turned to Yenu. His voice was good. It was the voice of a man who had been trained to address Drift Clade adolescents and had practiced.

"My name is Aurel Ket," he said. "I work for a cultural reinforcement program out of the Lattice Core. We are, frankly, in the business of stabilizing endangered cultural memory before it disperses. Missed-generation stones like yours are — I am sorry to be blunt — exactly the kind of stones we are commissioned to acquire. We pay above market. Significantly above. And we do not unmake them. We preserve them in a curated archive. They are heard. They are studied. They are *kept.*"

"Aurel," Tess said, with the tone of someone who had warned a man twice already.

"One moment, Tess." He did not look at her. He looked only at Yenu. "I will tell you the offer. You decide what you do with it. Your grandmother's voice would enter a permanent collection. She would be remembered, properly, in archives that do not crash. The Bleaching could not touch her there. She would also" — he hesitated, calibrating — "she would also become part of a cultural reinforcement field. Her recitation could help hold the public spaces people depend on, and be heard by many more lives than one family can reach."

"Aurel."

"I am almost done, Tess." He named a number. It was three times the high end of Tess's range. "Plus a stipend, paid annually to your pod, for the use rights. Indexed to inflation."

Yenu did not speak.

The two children Hol-Vren had moved were sleeping in the galley by now. Aurel could buy their beds back for them, and buy the next repair besides. Yenu looked at the case. He had spoken about her grandmother's voice; he had not mentioned the hollow.

"If I sign," she asked, "can I hear this stone as it was tuned for me?"

"You would have access to the collection."

"I mean this stone. The second register. Would it still be there?"

Aurel considered the question. "I can guarantee the recording and its provenance. I can't guarantee what civic use would do to a private tuning. The original would remain in our custody."

He was giving her a careful answer. She wished it were a different one.

"Then I can't sell it to you."

"The offer holds until second watch tomorrow," he said. "The field could do real good. So could the payment."

He set his card beside the case and left without asking again. Yenu put the card in her pocket. Three times Tess's high number was difficult to leave lying on a counter.

Tess closed the case and slid it back to her. "I can sound out two private buyers at the range I gave you. The stone stays with you. If either makes an offer, you can answer from the pod."

"Ask them."

"If the stone changes, the valuation changes."

Yenu latched the case. "I know."

"What will you do when you get home?"

"See my grandfather," Yenu said. "I haven't decided the rest."

"The tether-shuttle leaves in forty minutes," Tess said. "Eat something first."

## V. The Recitation in Return

Yenu read Aurel's card twice on the cup ride home. The payment was written in large type; permanent custody of the original stone was not. She could answer him before second watch tomorrow. Tess might find another buyer. Either sale could pay for the filament, and if neither happened, the two children would go on sleeping in the galley.

She opened the case's latch without lifting the lid. A faint trace of her grandmother's voice came through the wood. The hollow did not rise to meet her. At Tess's bench she had kept her hands away; here she kept the lid between them. Neither had been the listening the stone was made for.

*We carry only what we can put down,* her clade said. It did not tell her whether she could put down this stone or the pod's repair bill.

She closed the latch and watched the filament draw them home.

The cup got back to the pod shortly after a new first watch began. The pod was quiet. The galley light was on. The elder had left a cup of bitter green on the table, cold, untouched, in the polite Drift Clade way of saying *I waited, but I will not ask.*

She walked past it.

She went to her grandfather's sleeping-cell.

He was still alive. His eyes opened when she came in.

"Did you sell it," he said.

"No."

"How much would it bring?"

"Tess thinks twelve to fourteen cycles' wages. Enough for Hol-Vren's filament. A Consortium buyer offered three times the high end, but he would keep the original stone. I told him no. Tess is looking for another buyer."

Her grandfather turned his face toward the empty chair beside the bed. "Your mother would want the filament."

"I know."

"She might be right."

Yenu pulled the chair nearer. "You held this for me all those years. Did you ever think of selling it?"

"Last winter." He looked at her. "And after the galley wall softened. I was relieved each time it was still yours to decide. I was ashamed of the relief."

He rested. She did not fill the silence for him.

"I would like to hear her again," he said. "I have no right to ask. Tess told you what might happen if you listen?"

"She doesn't know. It might change. There might be nothing left to sell."

"Then don't do it for me." His hand moved toward the case and stopped short. "Give me a Yenu answer, not a clade answer."

She thought of the two children asleep in the galley and of her grandmother, whose voice she had heard only through a ring. Neither thought released her from the other.

"I want to hear what she chose to tell me," Yenu said. "With you. I know what it might cost."

She brought the case to the bedside. She unlatched it. She lifted the stone, for the first time, into her palm.

It was warm against her palm, as if it had been waiting to be held.

She had no tuning ring. At Tess's bench the ring had offered a trace while Yenu kept her hands away. Now she held the stone between herself and her grandfather, rested her thumb against its warm face, and answered the voice she had heard from a distance.

"I'm here," she said.

It sang.

The first register filled the cell. *Yen, Yen, Yen.* The sleeve, the hand on the back of the neck, and then words the ring had not given her: *The tuning chair rocks because I cut the near leg short. Your grandfather blames the floor. Don't let him plane the other three to match it.*

Her grandfather looked at the chair beside the bed. "I did blame the floor," he said. The leg had been short all Yenu's life.

The second register rose under it. The hollow. The cup-shape carved for Yenu was occupied at last, by Yenu listening, and it began to change.

She felt the stone shift, slightly, in her palm.

Tess's ring had shown her the hollow's edge. This listening filled it. Yenu did not know whether another stone tuned this way would change as this one had, but she felt the hollow close under her thumb. A *closed inheritance*, she thought. Whatever the name meant elsewhere, this stone had done its work.

She repeated the chair instruction under her breath, catching the dry amusement in her grandmother's voice. She could give those words to her mother. The voice settled into a recitation she could carry, rather than one more stone to keep sealed.

The stone in her palm went cool.

Tears stood in her grandfather's eyes. He did not wipe them.

"Thank you," he said. "That's enough."

"Yes."

"Yenu."

"Yes."

"I don't think I'll see another watch," he said. "The medic knows I want to stay here. Will you sit with me?"

"If you need her, I'll call."

"The bell is beside you."

"Recite me to your mother after. Get it right."

"I will."

"Get the part about the throttle right. It was honest miniaturization. People always remember the rest and forget that part."

"I will."

He closed his eyes.

She sat with him for the hour. Once he asked her to turn the chair so he could see its uneven leg. Otherwise they listened to the pod's low hum. The stone cooled in her hand.

His breathing stopped near the end of the hour. Yenu pressed the bell. The medic came, checked him, and left them the green light. He had died in his own bed.

Yenu stayed another twenty minutes. Then she carried the stone — not the case; the case stayed by the bed — down the corridor, past Hol-Vren's brace and the bowed corner, to the pod's outer membrane.

## VI. The Release

Skather-Ait's outer membrane held a thumb-sized port for releasing stones. Yenu had watched others use it. She opened the inner seal and set her grandmother's stone beside the outer one.

She held the cool stone in her palm.

The carrier was closed. No buyer could draw the memory from it now, and no price on Aurel's card could pay for the filament. She could put the stone on an ancestral shelf. For a moment she wanted to. Then she remembered the chair's short leg, a detail she could take to her mother without carrying a stone between them.

She closed the inner seal and opened the outer one. A puff of pod-air sent the stone into the Drift. She watched until she could no longer pick it out.

Her grandfather had called dark matter the kindest archive because it asked nothing of what it held. He had been speaking of memories. This was only a spent carrier.

She sealed the port and walked back past Hol-Vren's brace. The corner beyond it still bowed past yesterday's wax mark.

She walked to the galley.

Her mother was at the table. Two children slept on bedding against the galley wall, their boots tucked beneath their heads. The elder's untouched cup of bitter green sat between them.

"He died," Yenu said. "The medic came."

Her mother held the cold cup in both hands without lifting it. When she looked up, she saw Yenu's empty hands. "You released the stone."

"After we heard her."

"What did Tess offer?"

"A range. Twelve to fourteen cycles of wages, if she found a buyer. Aurel offered more."

Her mother looked toward the children. "I would have told you to sell it before you left. That's why the elder sent you past me."

"I know."

"I might have been right."

"You might have been."

"At the next first watch, Hol-Vren needs help with those bunks. The filament and retuning still cost eight cycles' wages."

"I'll be there."

Her mother set a hand on the cold cup. "Did she say anything? Your grandmother."

"She said she cut the tuning chair's leg short. She told me not to let him plane the others to match it."

For the first time that night her mother smiled, and then she covered her mouth. "He blamed the floor."

"He still did."

They sat with the cup between them. The children slept. The brace at the sleeping-cells would have to hold until the pod found another way to pay for the filament.

"He asked me to recite him to you," Yenu said.

Her mother nodded. Yenu began with his parents and the spine, then reached the part about Ait-Resh. "He did not remarry because he could not afford to," she said, giving the correction as he had given it to her.

The soft place beside the galley door firmed a fraction. The sleeping-cell brace remained where Hol-Vren had bolted it.

"Go on," her mother said.

Yenu did. She would help move the bunks at the next first watch.
