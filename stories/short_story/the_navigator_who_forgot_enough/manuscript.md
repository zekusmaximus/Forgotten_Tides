# THE NAVIGATOR WHO FORGOT ENOUGH

## I. The Petition

The petition arrived in a blue Canticle sleeve with no wax on it.

That was the first wrongness. Fleet orders came sealed, signed, warm with the
hand that wanted obedience. This one had been left on Lira Voss's desk between
morning release and the fourth-bell meal, as if paper could make itself innocent
by arriving quietly.

She read it once. Then she read the weight report beneath it.

Nim Orra: patient, Solace Hospice, advanced amnesia cascade. Personal
coherence low. Gravitational imprint light but stable under supervised
conditions. No active pilot anchors. No recoverable long-form autobiography.
Retains speech, choice response, humor, object recognition, and a reliable
preference for bitter tea.

At the bottom, in the assessor's careful hand:

Candidate may survive zero-coherence margin contact long enough to retrieve
distress name.

Lira set the page down.

Outside her office, Solace Hospice moved with its usual restraint. Carts passed
on soft wheels. A novice recited room names to the north corridor because the
north corridor had begun, in recent months, to answer more slowly. Somewhere, a
patient laughed once and stopped, either because the joke ended or because the
person who understood it had thinned out of reach.

The Void Regions lay beyond the station's outer sleeve. They did not loom. They
did not shine. The window over Lira's desk showed ordinary black, pricked by
stars with enough memory to burn. Past the mapped beacons, where those stars no
longer agreed on distance, there were places without narrative pressure. Matter
remained. Meaning did not.

The old atlas line said amnesiacs could navigate there freely.

Lira had always disliked that sentence.

Freely was not safely. Freely was what institutions called a body when they had
found a way to spend it without breaking an existing law.

The door chimed.

"Enter," she said.

The Canticle assessor came in without rank tabs. Solace required that much. He
still carried himself like a man waiting for a deck to salute.

"Remembrancer Voss."

"Assessor."

His eyes went to the open sleeve. "You have read it."

"I have."

"Then you understand the urgency."

"I understand the sentence."

He looked tired enough to be honest if pressed. Lira disliked that too. It was
easier to refuse monsters. Most institutions sent clerks with clean cuffs and
reasonable voices.

"A Canticle recovery skiff vanished at the near edge of the Void Regions three
pulls ago," he said. "No corridor residue. No beacon coherence. We received one
distress packet before the field flattened it."

"The packet contained a name."

"Part of one. Enough to know there is a person in the record. Not enough to know
who."

Lira looked at the petition again. "And you believe the missing syllables remain
inside a zero-coherence margin."

"Believe is too strong."

"Convenient."

"Possible." His mouth tightened. "The families have seven bodies and no names.
The skiff manifest went with the drive. If the packet came from a survivor, the
name may be the last coherent fact they made."

"Facts are not free because they are last."

"No."

That answer was too quick. He had expected the line. Perhaps he had practiced
against it in the corridor.

Lira folded the weight report once. "Why Nim?"

"Because every trained navigator we put near the margin dissociated within
eleven breaths. Their anchors caught on the absence. The more they remembered,
the harder the field pulled."

"And Nim remembers less."

"Nim's imprint is low enough not to hook."

"A clean operational phrase."

"An ugly one," he said. "Still true."

The room settled around them. A shelf ticked as its wood remembered a different
humidity. On the wall, above the patient ledgers, Solace doctrine rested in
dark-cut letters:

*Spend only what you can afford. Preserve what must endure.*

Lira had watched patients read that line as accusation, release, comfort,
permission. The stone did not change. People did.

"Nim is under Solace care," she said.

"We are requesting cooperation."

"You are requesting a person."

"We are requesting a consent audit for a volunteer."

"Has Nim volunteered?"

The assessor hesitated.

There it was.

"Nim agreed to hear the request," he said.

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

Again the clean answer. Again the tiredness.

Lira stood. "Then we begin there."

## II. The Person Light Enough

Nim Orra was in the west garden, teaching a cup to be a cup.

The west garden had no soil. Soil carried too many embedded seasons for the
patients nearest release; roots could become arguments. Solace kept this garden
in low trays of mineral felt, with shallow water and gray-leaved plants that
held memory poorly. Their fragrance vanished between one breath and the next.

Nim sat cross-legged beside the tray with a white cup in both hands.

"It is for tea," Nim told the cup. "Not for nails. Not for buttons. Tea."

The cup made the faint ceramic note of an object accepting instruction.

"Good," Nim said gravely.

Lira stopped at the threshold. The assessor stopped behind her.

Nim looked up. Their face was narrow and ageless in the way advanced lightness
could make a face: not young, not old, only unclaimed by enough continuity to
settle. The hospice robe hung from their shoulders without quite believing in
weight. Their hair was clipped short. Lira knew, with the professional part of
her mind, that she had cut it two days ago because Nim had forgotten the use of
a comb and become frightened by the teeth.

With the unprofessional part of her mind, she knew Nim had laughed when the
first lock fell.

"Lira," Nim said.

The name landed. It still had force. Good.

"Nim."

Nim's gaze moved to the assessor. "Blue sleeve."

"Canticle," Lira said.

"They count things."

"Yes."

"Have they come to count me?"

The assessor inhaled. Lira lifted one hand without looking at him. He kept the
answer in his mouth.

Lira knelt on the stone path. "They have come to ask something dangerous."

"Do I like dangerous things?"

"You like bitter tea and locked doors. Your opinion on danger changes."

Nim considered this with evident seriousness. "Locked doors are honest. They
say no with their whole body."

"Usually."

"What is the dangerous thing?"

The assessor shifted. Lira let him speak. It mattered that the request not wear
her voice.

"A distress packet was damaged near the Void Regions," he said. "It carried
part of a name. We need someone to enter the margin and bring back the rest."

Nim looked past him toward the window. The garden had one narrow viewport,
mostly shuttered. Stars showed in a line like pinholes.

"Why me?"

"Because the margin harms heavy minds quickly," he said. "You are lighter than
our navigators."

Nim smiled a little. "That sounds like a compliment that has lost its manners."

"It is not a compliment."

"No. I thought not."

Lira set the folded report on the stone between them. "Nim, do you understand
what the Void Regions are?"

"Places with no memory."

"Yes."

"Places where things stop being held in the world."

"Yes."

"Have I been there?"

"No."

"Have I asked to go?"

"Not yet."

That pleased them. Nim's smile warmed and vanished. "Then I am still polite."

The assessor opened his sleeve and withdrew a thin slate. "The crossing would
be brief. A tethered excursion from the outer lock, no corridor formation. You
would remain in voice contact with Remembrancer Voss. You would listen for the
name and repeat it."

"Listen with ears?"

"With the sensorium ring."

Nim touched their brow, where no ring rested now. "Will it hurt?"

The assessor looked at Lira.

Lira did not rescue him.

"Yes," he said.

"Will I die?"

"We do not expect that."

Nim nodded. "That means maybe."

"Yes."

"Will I forget?"

The assessor's throat moved. "Likely."

"What?"

"We cannot know."

Nim turned the cup in their hands. "That is the trouble with forgetting. It does
not send a packing list."

The witness stone at Lira's belt remained dark. There was no audit yet. Only a
conversation.

"If I say yes," Nim asked, "who gets heavier?"

The assessor blinked.

Lira closed her eyes briefly. Nim still did that, sometimes: stepped past all
procedure to the real weight-bearing wall.

"The name does," Lira said. "The families, perhaps. The Fleet record. Whoever
made the distress packet, if there is enough of them left to be held by a name."

"And who gets lighter?"

No one answered quickly.

Nim looked at Lira.

"I do," they said.

"Yes."

"Maybe you."

Lira felt the garden settle. The cup in Nim's hands gave a tiny click.

"Maybe," she said.

The assessor's gaze sharpened, but he kept silent. Good. He was learning, or he
was afraid. Both could look like respect from a distance.

"I want tea," Nim said.

"Now?"

"Before I decide. I make better choices when my mouth remembers bitterness."

Lira stood. "Then tea first."

The assessor almost objected. Solace stone, Solace air, Solace silence pressed
around him until he remembered where he was.

Tea first.

The universe could wait one cup longer before using someone.

## III. The Audit

They held the audit in the same chamber where patients released anchors, though
Nim had no anchors left that answered reliably.

That was the second wrongness. The room had been built to witness chosen
forgetting, not chosen exposure. Its low stone ledges were worn by pilots,
clerics, citizens, and the occasional commander who arrived certain they wanted
to let go and spent three hours discovering the difference between relief and
absence.

Nim sat opposite Lira with the tea cup between both palms. The sensorium ring
rested dark on the reliquary tray. The witness stone sat beside it.

The assessor waited outside the seal. Lira had not invited him in.

"He looks like a closed door," Nim said.

"He has had a difficult week."

"Doors often have."

Lira touched the witness stone. It woke to a dull gray, ready to hear.

"Nim Orra," she said. "This is not a release rite. This is a consent audit for a
requested margin crossing. Do you know your name?"

Nim's eyes lowered to the slate in their lap.

"Nim Orra," they read.

"Without the slate, if you can."

Nim closed their eyes. The room held its breath around them.

"Nim," they said.

"And the second name?"

Their brow furrowed. "It begins in the mouth and leaves before the teeth."

"Orra."

"Orra," Nim repeated, relieved and embarrassed. "Yes."

Lira marked the audit. "Do you know where you are?"

"Solace Hospice."

"Do you know why you are here?"

"Because I forgot too much for ordinary rooms."

The witness stone warmed by a single degree.

"Do you know what Canticle has asked?"

"They lost a name near the empty place. They want me to fetch it because I am
thin enough to slip through."

Not the assessor's language. Not Lira's. Nim's own, for now.

"Do you understand the crossing may cost memory, identity, language, or your
remaining connection to people who know you?"

Nim looked at the tea. "Say that again."

Lira did.

Nim listened with the full attention of someone receiving weather.

"Again," they said.

Lira said it a third time.

The stone brightened, not because the answer had come, but because the question
had weight.

"What connection?" Nim asked.

Lira held still.

"To me," she said. "To this place. To the fact that you prefer bitter tea. To
the part of you that teaches cups. To anything still making a shape called Nim
Orra."

"Ah." Nim's thumb moved along the cup handle. "That is more than I thought."

"Yes."

"The report says I have little to lose."

Lira did not ask how Nim knew. Patients saw what institutions thought hidden.
Thin minds slipped through procedural cracks.

"The report is wrong."

"It says low personal coherence."

"That is not the same as little to lose."

"What is the difference?"

Lira looked at the witness stone. It would keep this. It would keep the question
longer than Nim might.

"A thin thread can still be the thread holding a bridge."

Nim absorbed that. "Am I a bridge?"

"Today, perhaps."

"To whom?"

"That is what worries me."

Nim drank the last of the tea and made a face. "Good. Terrible. Good."

The cup held its shape.

"Why would you say yes?" Lira asked.

Nim leaned back against the ledge. "Because I know what it is to hear only part
of myself."

The witness stone brightened.

"Because when people speak around me and cannot find the right name for what I
am, the air gets cold."

Brighter.

"Because if someone is out there becoming less than a packet, and their name is
still knocking, I think the door should be opened."

The stone turned milk-white.

Consent, then. Or something near enough to frighten her.

Lira continued. "Do you choose this because Canticle asked you?"

"No."

"Because Solace asked you?"

"No."

"Because you believe your life has less value than the name?"

Nim's face changed. Offense arrived cleanly, a welcome sign of density.

"No."

"Because you want to die?"

"No."

"Because you want to be useful?"

Nim hesitated.

The stone dimmed.

Lira waited.

"A little," Nim admitted.

"Useful to whom?"

"Anyone." Their voice thinned on the last syllable. "No. That is not a good
answer. Wait."

They pressed both hands flat to the floor. Lira recognized the gesture. She had
taught it to them six days ago during a room-fright. The stone floor is here.
The hands are here. The person between them is here.

"Useful to the person whose name it is," Nim said at last. "Not to the Fleet.
Not first."

The witness stone recovered its light.

Lira let out a breath she had not meant to hold.

"Do you understand you may return unable to reaffirm this choice?"

"Yes."

"Do you understand that if you cannot reaffirm it, the record may be all that
remains of your consent?"

Nim smiled without amusement. "That is already how mornings work."

Lira's hand tightened around the stylus.

"Do you choose the crossing?"

Nim looked at the wall. Their eyes found *afford*. Then *endure*.

"I choose to try," they said. "I do not choose to be spent."

The witness stone flared.

That distinction entered the room like a bell.

Lira marked it in her own hand.

## IV. The Margin

The outer lock had been built for maintenance crews, not rites. Solace had made
what corrections it could. Someone had carved the doctrine into the inner
hatch, smaller than in the chambers, so a person leaving could touch each word
with one hand.

Nim touched *spend*. Then *afford*. Then stopped.

"What if I cannot afford it after all?"

Lira stood beside them with the sensorium ring held in both hands. "Then we
bring you back."

"What if I cannot tell you?"

"Then I watch for what your words cannot carry."

Nim accepted this with a nod. It was not comfort. Comfort would have been a lie
with soft edges. This was only a task.

The assessor waited at the console with two Solace novices and a Canticle
technician whose hands never stopped moving. The tether spool lay between them:
physical cable, voice line, sensorium filament. No corridor. No memory drive.
Nothing to thicken a path. A path would only give the Void something to unmake.

"Three breaths to margin," the technician said.

"No countdown," Lira said.

The technician looked up.

"This is not a launch."

The assessor spoke quietly. "Do as she says."

Lira set the ring on Nim's brow. The metal looked too heavy for them until it
touched skin. Then it seemed to forget its own burden.

"Name what remains," Lira said.

Nim glanced at the slate strapped to their wrist. "Nim. Solace. Bitter tea. Cup
for tea, not nails. Lira."

Each word answered in the ring: faint, but present.

"Again."

"Nim. Solace. Bitter tea. Cup for tea. Lira."

"Again."

"Nim. Solace. Bitter tea. Lira."

Lira did not let the loss show on her face.

"Good."

The outer hatch opened.

Black waited beyond it. Not dark. Dark had texture; dark remembered light. This
was only the absence of claims.

The tether drew Nim out by degrees.

At first nothing happened. The station hum continued. The novices breathed too
loudly. The assessor gripped the console edge until his knuckles whitened.

Then the voice line flattened.

"Nim," Lira said.

"Here."

The word came back without echo.

"Name what remains."

"Nim. Solace. Bitter..." A pause. "Bitter is a taste."

"Yes."

"Tea is the cup's reason."

"Yes."

The tether extended another meter.

The sensorium feed showed no corridor geometry, no eddy curvature, no drift
pattern. Instruments disliked absence. They tried to make numbers and produced
hesitation.

"Margin contact," the technician whispered.

The room's meaning changed.

The console was still a console, but for one breath Lira could not remember why
hands belonged on it. The hatch became a circle without promise. The novices
blurred into robes, robes into vertical pale. The assessor's blue sleeve lost
the authority it had carried and became color, then less than color.

Lira pressed her palm to the hatch frame.

Metal. Solace. Patient.

"Nim."

No answer.

The tether did not jerk. That was worse.

"Nim Orra," Lira said.

Static breathed through the line. Not sound. A pressure where sound had been.

Then Nim's voice, very small: "Too many edges gone."

"Come back one meter."

"No."

The witness stone at Lira's belt went hot.

Consent had not ended. Neither had danger.

"Nim, come back one meter."

"The name is near."

"You do not have to retrieve it."

"I know."

"Say what remains."

Silence.

"Nim."

"I am..." A pause long enough for the room to tilt. "I am the one hearing."

The assessor shut his eyes.

Lira understood then what the report had not. Nim's lightness did not let them
pass untouched. It let the Void reach deeper before there was enough self to
resist. A heavy mind struck the absence and shattered at the surface. Nim sank.

The name was not waiting like an object. It was caught in the only place a name
could catch: in a relation. Someone had called for someone. The packet had
carried the call until the margin stripped everything but need.

To bring it back, Nim had to become the listener.

And a listener needed weight.

Lira felt the first pull in her own memory. Not pain. Reclassification.

Nim in the west garden, instructing the cup. The recollection loosened. It did
not vanish at once. It became less hers, as if someone had moved it from a lived
room into an archive drawer.

"No," Lira said softly.

The Void did not answer. It had no manners and no malice.

The second pull took Nim's laugh when the cut hair fell across their knees. Lira
kept the fact, lost the sound. Her mind supplied a blank where warmth had been.

"Remembrancer?" the assessor said.

"Quiet."

She could sever the tether. She could bring Nim back without the name, perhaps
with enough of them remaining to teach cups again. She could preserve what was
in her care and let the distress packet flatten into namelessness.

Nim had said: I choose to try. I do not choose to be spent.

The distinction held like a blade.

Lira leaned into the voice line. "Nim, listen to me."

"Lira?"

The name came back uncertain, but it came.

"You are not the name. You are not the packet. You are not Canticle's need."

"I know." Then, smaller: "I do not know. Say it again."

Lira said it again.

The third pull took the afternoon Nim had refused sweet tea with theatrical
disgust. Lira remembered writing the preference in the chart. She no longer
remembered why she had smiled.

Relationship was memory under tension. The Void could use it.

"You may return without it," Lira said. "That is still a kept choice."

"Someone called."

"Yes."

"No one should call forever."

Lira closed her eyes.

There was the cost, plain at last. Not rescue. Not restoration. Only ending one
unanswered call by thinning the caller, the listener, and the witness who held
the line between them.

"If you bring it," Lira said, "bring only the name. Not the fear. Not the route.
Not the silence around it."

"Only the name."

"Only enough."

The tether line went slack.

For one terrible breath the instruments found Nim's weight and measured nothing.

Then the voice returned, carrying syllables that were not for the open air.
Lira heard them. The witness stone heard them. The assessor heard enough to
cover his mouth with one hand.

The name entered the room and made the room heavier.

The cost followed.

Nim screamed once, but the scream did not know whether it belonged to a body or
a signal. Lira hit the recall. The tether snapped taut. The technician swore
under his breath and hauled with both hands. The novices began the room litany,
naming hatch, hinge, rail, floor.

Lira named Nim.

She named Solace.

She named bitter tea and cup and the hand that had held it.

On the fifth repetition she could not remember the hand.

On the sixth, Nim crossed the hatch.

## V. What Came Back

Nim returned breathing.

At first that was the whole report.

They lay on the lock floor with the sensorium ring dark on their brow and frost
on their eyelashes, though the Void was not cold. Cold was only how the body
translated absence after the fact.

Lira knelt beside them. "Nim."

Their eyes moved.

"Nim Orra."

Their gaze caught on her face with no recognition in it.

The assessor stood behind Lira, silent. The name he had wanted was sealed in the
witness stone now. He had not asked for it to be played back. Not yet. Some
decency had survived the crossing, or some fear.

"Where?" Nim whispered.

"Solace Hospice."

The words did not settle.

"What is that?"

Lira put one hand flat on the floor where Nim could see it. "A place that keeps
people from being used too quickly."

Nim thought about this. "Did it keep me?"

The question struck harder than accusation.

"Not enough," Lira said.

The assessor flinched. Lira did not look away from Nim.

"Did I agree?"

"Yes."

"Was I... clear?"

Lira reached for the witness stone at her belt. It was no longer hot. Its
surface had gone clear as old ice.

"You were clear enough to frighten me," she said.

Nim seemed to accept that. Their hand rose, touched the cup-shaped absence of
the sensorium ring, then fell.

"Do I know you?"

Lira opened her mouth.

The answer should have been easy. Yes. I am Lira. I have sat with you through
six dawns. I know how you take tea. I know you teach cups their purpose. I know
you laugh when frightened by harmless things because laughter makes a rail for
the room.

Most of that was in the ledger now. Some of it might have been true. The shape
remained. The gravity had gone.

She knew Nim clinically. She knew the record. She knew the duty.

The bond answered weakly, like a struck cup in another chamber.

"A little," she said.

Nim nodded with grave sympathy, as if Lira were the patient. Perhaps she was.

"We can start there."

The assessor turned away then. He moved to the console and stood with his head
bowed over the sealed playback. He had the name. It would go into a Fleet ledger
and then to families who would finally have a sound to gather around. A body
could be remembered. A death could become particular. Grief could find its
handle.

That mattered.

It did not cleanse the method.

When the medics lifted Nim, their robe barely shifted the stretcher springs.
Lira watched for some private sign of the person who had entered the lock: a
preference, a joke, a glance toward the cup waiting on the bench.

Nim looked at the cup without interest.

"Tea?" Lira asked.

"Is that what it is for?"

"Yes."

"Then it is a good cup."

No recognition. Only logic.

Lira turned away before the loss could become expression.

The dawn audit took three hours.

The assessor gave his testimony without ornament. He did not say successful. He
said recovered. He did not say minimal loss. He said ongoing assessment. Twice,
he began to call Nim a candidate and corrected himself to patient.

Lira recorded every correction.

At the end, he remained seated across from her in the audit chamber, the sealed
witness stone between them.

"There will be pressure to repeat this," he said.

"Yes."

"If the families learn how the name was retrieved-"

"They should learn."

He looked up sharply.

"Not the syllables," Lira said. "Those belong where they are going. But the cost
must be recorded."

"It will make the Fleet look predatory."

"The Fleet was predatory."

He accepted the word like a man taking a measured wound. "And Solace?"

Lira looked toward the west garden through the chamber slit. From here she could
see only the gray leaves, their poor memory a mercy in the artificial light.

"Solace opened the door."

"Nim chose."

"Yes."

"You witnessed it."

"Yes."

"Was that not enough?"

The witness stone sat clear and hard between them. Consent valid. Name
recovered. Patient alive.

A perfect procedural sentence. A terrible moral one.

"It was enough to proceed," Lira said. "It was not enough to make proceeding
clean."

He covered his eyes with thumb and forefinger. For a moment rank, institution,
and argument fell away, leaving only a tired person who had helped spend another
tired person.

"What do I tell command?"

"Tell them amnesiac lightness is not a method. It is an injury."

He lowered his hand.

"Tell them the margin did not spare Nim because Nim had less to lose. It took
what remained with greater precision."

The assessor nodded once.

"And the name?"

"Tell them a name came back heavier than the person who carried it."

After he left, Lira replayed the audit alone.

Not the distress name. That remained sealed under Fleet and Solace witness. She
played Nim's consent.

The stone gave back Nim's voice: I choose to try. I do not choose to be spent.

Lira listened three times. On the fourth, she stopped. Repetition could preserve
a thing or wear it smooth. The difference depended on why the hand kept
returning.

She wrote the final ledger in her own hand.

*Canticle petition granted under protest. Patient Nim Orra consented with
documented instability and repeated comprehension checks. Void margin crossing
retrieved one distress name. Retrieval caused measurable loss of patient
self-boundary, loss of prior preference recall, and attenuation of witnessed
patient-bond in attending Remembrancer. No memory restored. No route recovered.
No protocol established.*

She let the ink dry.

In the west garden, Nim sat beside the mineral tray with the white cup in their
hands.

Lira stood at the threshold. She did not know whether she had watched this
before. The chart said she had. The room did not give back the feeling.

Nim looked up.

"Lira," they read from the slate.

"Yes."

"Nim," they read next, touching their own chest.

"Yes."

They considered the cup.

"It is for tea," Nim said.

"Yes."

"Not nails."

"No."

"Not buttons."

"No."

The cup made a faint ceramic note. It was not memory, not yet. Only an object
accepting a use.

Nim smiled politely, as one stranger might smile to another at the beginning of
a long and difficult kindness.

Lira sat beside them.

Beyond the shuttered window, the Void Regions remained untroubled by what had
been taken from them, and the recovered name began its heavy journey home.
