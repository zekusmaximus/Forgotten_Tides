# The Inventory of Absence

## I. Zero Lot

The manifest arrived before the containers.

Toma Vey noticed because manifests liked to arrive late. Freight had mass, seals,
temperature requirements, insurance clauses, and people who could be blamed if it
went missing. Paperwork had none of those virtues. On Heavonly's lower decks,
paperwork usually appeared after the cargo had already dented a bay wall and three
handlers had argued over whose count was wrong.

This one entered the system six minutes early.

The intake board woke above Toma's station and displayed a line with no weight.

LOT HN-0-7741. QUARANTINE TRANSFER. EMPTY CONTAINMENT. NO CARGO.

He stared at the last two words until the board dimmed to save itself the
embarrassment.

"No cargo does not need a bay," he said.

The board brightened again.

BAY TWELVE.

Toma marked the lot on his wrist slate because the lower-deck boards were loyal
to whoever paid for their maintenance, and this one carried a Consortium compliance
tag behind the Authority routing stamp. Not illegal. Not uncommon. Not comforting.

Heavonly received everything eventually. Pilgrims, senators, counterfeit reliquary
filings, spoiled protein sheets, diplomatic gifts with too much insurance, and
small frightened families from worlds whose names did not survive customs. The
upper levels called Heavonly a political hub and gateway to three galaxies. The
lower decks called it a throat. Things entered, were squeezed into countable
shapes, and left useful.

Toma worked in the part of the throat where objects first became someone else's
problem.

At third bell, the containers arrived.

There were seventeen of them.

Toma counted twice with his eyes, once with his slate, and once the way his first
freight teacher had taught him: by the pauses handlers made while steering. A
loaded train had a rhythm. Empty beds ran eager. Dangerous cargo made people slow
down even when they pretended not to know why.

Seventeen units crossed the threshold into Bay Twelve.

The manifest corrected itself to sixteen.

Toma lifted his hand. "Stop the line."

The lead handler looked back from the tug. She had one boot on the bay stripe and
one in the hall, which meant the cargo was still intake's problem, and she knew it.

"Why?"

"Count drift."

"On empties?"

"Especially on empties."

She did not laugh. Lower-deck workers survived by respecting sentences that sounded
foolish until they killed someone.

The tug stopped. The bay floor took the weight of the containers and settled too
far. Not visibly enough for the handlers to step back, but enough for Toma's ankle
to notice. The painted TWELVE on the far wall blurred at the edges, as if the bay
were considering whether a number was really necessary.

He counted again.

Seventeen containers. Sixteen in the manifest. No cargo.

Each container was waist-high, matte white, sealed at the corners with Consortium
memory tape. The tape carried no text. Just a gray emblem and a clearance level
that made Toma's slate refuse to display the origin field.

The lead handler wiped her mouth with the back of her glove. "Quarantine lot?"

"Administrative."

"That is worse than medical."

"Usually."

The nearest container made no sound. That was expected. What worried Toma was that
the bay made no sound around it. Bay Twelve was never quiet. Fans coughed. Lifts
complained. Old brackets ticked under cargo shifts. Around the sealed lot, the
ordinary noises thinned to a polite distance.

Toma stepped over the bay stripe.

His wrist slate blinked.

LOT HN-0-7741. QUARANTINE TRANSFER. EMPTY CONTAINMENT. NO CARGO.

COUNT: 15.

"No," he said.

The slate accepted the correction, then forgot it before his thumb left the glass.

The lead handler saw his face. "Do we back it out?"

He looked at the containers, then at the hall behind them. Three other freight
trains waited there, all real enough to be irritated. Food stock for Level Seven.
Mirror parts for upper concourse repairs. A sealed Archivist pouch riding in a
lead cradle. If he blocked the throat too long, upper traffic would notice. If
upper traffic noticed, someone with cleaner cuffs would ask why a lower-deck
auditor had delayed no cargo.

"Set chocks," Toma said. "Manual count marks. Chalk, not board."

The handler nodded and sent two crew for the chalk box.

Heavonly's systems could forget numbers. Chalk had the advantage of being stupid.

## II. The Count

Toma wrote numbers on the floor, not the containers.

One through seventeen. Big block figures, white against black bay paint. He wrote
slowly, keeping his wrist slate turned away so it would not try to be helpful.
The handlers watched from the stripe. One of them muttered the count with him,
which made the room feel heavier by a usable degree.

Thirteen resisted the chalk.

The line skipped under his hand, leaving a pale smear where a number should have
been. Toma wrote it again. The second version held as long as he looked at it.

"Say it," he told the handlers.

"Thirteen," the lead handler said.

"Again."

"Thirteen."

The chalk settled.

Not good. Better.

Toma stood and checked the intake board.

LOT HN-0-7741. QUARANTINE TRANSFER. EMPTY CONTAINMENT. NO CARGO.

COUNT: 0.

The board had given up on compromise and returned to doctrine.

His slate chimed with a compliance note.

Do not open. Do not weigh. Do not sample. Do not classify as cargo. Transfer to
restricted disposal queue after intake acknowledgement.

There was no origin. Only a route sleeve: mid-world quarantine, inward corridor,
Heavonly receiving authority, Consortium compliance.

Mid-world. Not light enough for floaters in the drains. Not old enough to be
politically heavy. The kind of world that supplied workers, landcraft, and
statistics in convenient quantities.

Toma had processed many such shipments. Grain. Machine parts. People with stamped
papers and too many bags. Memory stones packed in private cases, always heavier
than the hands that carried them.

He had never processed no cargo in seventeen sealed units.

The bay lights dimmed.

One handler whispered, "Toma."

On the far wall, TWELVE had become TWE. The last letters had not fallen away like
paint. They had become undecided. The bay remembered it belonged somewhere in the
lower decks. It remembered intake. It remembered freight. It was less certain of
which freight, which bay, which number.

Memory generated coherence. Forgetting did not ask permission before starting with
labels.

Toma opened a manual exception.

RECEIVED AS: he typed.

The field offered suggestions.

Food stock. Machine part. Reliquary. Civic record. Personal effects. Waste.

He typed: absence.

The field cleared itself.

He typed: empty containment, physical.

The field cleared itself.

He typed: sealed lot, count seventeen, lowered local coherence.

The field accepted the words, displayed them for one heartbeat, then replaced them
with:

NO CARGO.

The lead handler crossed her arms. "System won't take it?"

"System takes what it has a category for."

"Make a category."

"I am lower freight. I can make a delay. Categories come from upstairs."

"Upstairs thinks cargo levitates into their speeches."

Toma almost smiled. Almost. Then the fifteenth chalk mark faded.

He crouched and wrote it again.

The action brought back a memory so abruptly that for a moment Bay Twelve vanished.
He was twelve years old in a storage room under a neighborhood bakery, counting
crates for his aunt because the shop board had failed and the delivery clerk had
insisted the missing flour must be a display error. His aunt had put chalk in his
hand.

Systems count what they are built to count, she had said. Hands count what will
be missed.

He remembered the flour dust on her sleeve. He remembered thinking the sentence
was too important for crates and too obvious for adulthood.

The memory had weight. Not much. Enough.

Fifteen held.

Toma stood carefully.

"No one leaves the bay alone," he said. "If you step out, tell someone what you
saw before you cross the stripe."

The youngest handler swallowed. "Why?"

"Because the lot is trying to become administratively true."

No cargo.

No count.

No handler.

It was not malice. Malice would have been easier. The containers sat in their row,
sealed and quiet, teaching the systems around them that absence was a valid answer.

## III. Compliance

The compliance call came from the upper levels with no image.

That meant either humility or threat. Toma had never known upper systems to pay
for the first.

"Freight Auditor Vey," the voice said. "Bay Twelve shows intake delay on Lot
HN-0-7741."

"Bay Twelve shows several things badly."

"The lot is classified as empty containment."

"It is physically present."

"Presence is not cargo."

Toma looked at the seventeen units, then at the board insisting on zero. "It is
when it occupies a bay."

"Do not weigh, open, sample, or reclassify."

"I did not ask to open it."

"You attempted an exception."

"The exception failed."

"Good."

There it was. Not a mistake, then. A preference.

The voice continued. "Acknowledge receipt and transfer to restricted disposal.
Your delay is causing lower-deck congestion."

Toma looked through the bay mouth. The congestion was real. Three delayed trains,
four impatient crews, one food supervisor silently inventing new ways to hate him.
Heavonly's lower decks were a body with narrow arteries. A clot in one bay became
hunger six levels away.

"What am I acknowledging?"

"Receipt."

"Of what?"

"Empty containment."

"How many?"

The call paused.

Not long. Long enough.

"The manifest indicates no cargo."

"How many containers?"

"Count is not required for no cargo."

Toma felt the bay dip again. The floor under the sealed lot smoothed out one
chalk mark while everyone watched. Twelve went first. An easy number to lose.
Even, quiet, forgettable.

Toma pressed his thumb hard against his slate edge until the pressure hurt.

"I have seventeen physical containers in Bay Twelve."

"That statement is unsupported by manifest."

"The manifest is unsupported by the floor."

The lead handler made a small approving sound and stopped when Toma glanced at her.

The compliance voice cooled. "Auditor Vey, you are instructed to acknowledge and
release."

"Send an Archivist witness."

"Not necessary."

"Then send a disposal captain willing to sign count responsibility."

"Responsibility cannot attach to zero cargo."

The sentence tried to settle. Toma felt it enter the bay, polite and poisonous.
Responsibility could not attach. Count was not required. No cargo.

If he let the sentence stand, the containers would leave the bay without ever
having become part of Heavonly's memory. Restricted disposal would take them into
some clean upper queue. The lower deck would remember a delay, maybe. A nuisance.
No lot. No count. No origin. No one responsible for the thing that made Bay Twelve
forget its own number.

He opened the exception field again.

The compliance voice sharpened. "Auditor Vey."

"I am still here."

The field cleared twice before he touched it.

RECEIVED AS:

He scrolled past food stock, machine part, reliquary, civic record.

Personal effects.

The field waited.

Personal effects required an owner. Heavonly's system would carry almost anything
if a person signed their identity under it. Lost coats. Dead workers' tool rolls.
Unclaimed memory stones. A child's box of birthday tokens that had crossed three
corridors and arrived after the family had moved on.

Personal effects were inefficient, sentimental, legally annoying, and therefore
difficult to erase.

Toma selected the field.

OWNER:

He did not type his name yet.

"If I file the lot under personal effects," he said, "the system keeps count."

The lead handler stared at him. "Under whose?"

The compliance line went silent.

That was answer enough.

Toma looked at the sealed containers. They were not his. That was the moral
problem. They belonged to whoever had been made into emptiness, if that was what
had happened. They belonged to a place that might already be too administratively
quiet to claim them.

Filing them under his name would be false.

It would also be less false than zero.

"Get me a witness," he told the handler.

"Archivist?"

"Anyone with a live mouth. Then an Archivist if we can make upstairs dislike the
noise."

The handler stepped to the bay mouth and began calling for names.

Toma typed:

OWNER: TOMA VEY.

The field accepted him immediately. Systems loved a living person to blame.

## IV. Personal Effects

The first cost was small.

His lunch credit vanished.

Toma saw the notice appear on his slate just as the personal-effects record opened.
Meal allocation unavailable. Employee status pending cargo review.

He laughed once, too quietly for anyone else to hear.

Bay Twelve did not change. That would have made the choice theatrical, and reality
rarely had the decency. The containers remained sealed. The chalk marks remained
mostly visible. The intake board still preferred zero.

But the personal-effects field held.

OWNER: TOMA VEY.

ITEM COUNT:

He typed 17.

The system flickered. Sixteen. Seventeen. Zero. Seventeen.

"Say it," he told the bay.

By then there were seven lower-deck workers at the stripe: handlers, the delayed
food supervisor, a mirror-parts clerk, and an old recycler who had come because
someone said Bay Twelve was being strange and old recyclers collected useful
strangeness.

"Seventeen," the lead handler said.

"Seventeen," the others repeated.

The number held.

DESCRIPTION:

Toma typed: Sealed absence-bearing containers received from quarantined mid-world
route under Consortium compliance tag. Physical units present. Manifest denies
cargo. Local coherence reduced.

The system highlighted absence-bearing in red and suggested deletion.

He deleted only the hyphen.

absence bearing containers.

The field accepted ugly grammar more readily than clean truth. He let it. Style
was for records that already believed him.

Compliance came back on the line with a second voice behind it. "Auditor Vey, you
are misusing personal-effects custody."

"Correct."

"Remove the filing."

"No."

"You are assuming personal liability for restricted material."

"No," Toma said. "I am assuming personal liability for the count."

The distinction mattered to him. It did not matter to the line.

His slate updated again.

RESIDENCE ACCESS: VERIFY AT LOCAL DESK.

He frowned.

The youngest handler saw it. "What?"

"Nothing."

Not nothing. The system had attached the lot to his personnel identity and found
the attachment distasteful. It was starting at the edges. Meal allocation. Residence
access. Small institutional memories that made a worker easy to house, feed, and
ignore.

He kept typing.

CHAIN OF CUSTODY:

Toma Vey, lower freight audit.

Witnesses:

The field asked for approved names.

"Names," he said.

One by one, the workers gave them.

The food supervisor hesitated before hers. Toma did not blame her. Witness was a
hook. Hooks held. Hooks caught.

When the last name entered, Bay Twelve's far wall recovered the LVE in TWELVE.
The full word held unsteadily. Toma had never felt grateful to a wall before.
He decided not to start now.

The old recycler pointed at the nearest container. "Seal's sweating."

It was not moisture. It was frost without cold, a pale grain forming along the
memory tape. One flake lifted and drifted away from the unit.

For a moment it hung in the air with no visible means of support.

Tiny. White. Lighter than dust.

It turned toward the bay lights, then forgot the direction halfway and wandered
toward the ceiling.

No one moved.

Toma watched the small thing drift above the sealed lot.

He knew the word for it from maintenance briefings and lower-deck jokes. Floater.
Usually harmless. Usually a nuisance. Usually not inside a sealed quarantine lot
from a mid-world route.

"Do not touch it," he said.

The lead handler's voice came out flat. "Was that cargo?"

Toma looked back at the personal-effects record.

The system still said seventeen.

"No," he said. "That was what cargo becomes when no one counts it in time."

It was a guess. He hated guesses in records. He did not write it down.

Instead he opened a secondary note.

Visible zero-memory residue observed after custody attachment. Do not classify as
proof of contents without Archivist review.

The note held.

The compliance line ended.

Five minutes later, an Archivist novice arrived out of breath from the upper lifts.
She wore her robe over dock boots, which made Toma like her immediately and trust
her not at all.

"Who filed this under personal effects?" she asked.

Toma raised his hand.

She looked at the board, the chalk, the containers, the floating speck near the
lights, and the workers at the stripe.

"That was irregular," she said.

"It was zero before."

The novice's face changed.

Not fear. Recognition. The kind institutions disliked because it happened before
permission.

"Show me the record," she said.

## V. Intake

The Archivist did not open the containers.

That was the first thing she did right.

She walked the row with a witness stone in one hand and Toma's slate in the other.
At each chalk number she stopped long enough for the stone to hear the workers say
the count. By the end, the stone had gone from dull gray to a thin, cold white.

"Seventeen physical units," she said.

The intake board displayed:

COUNT: 17.

Then:

COUNT: 0.

Then:

COUNT: 17, contested.

"Contested is better than gone," Toma said.

"Usually." The novice looked at his slate. "You used your own identity."

"It was available."

"So is an airlock, in many rooms."

"I did not have your witness stone."

"You have it now."

She sounded angry. Good. Anger had more weight than courtesy if the person holding
it knew where to put it.

The floating speck reached the ceiling and passed halfway through a conduit label
before stopping. The label now read OX instead of OXYGEN. The old recycler threw
a rag at it. The speck dodged without intention and vanished into a vent.

No one said anything for three breaths.

The Archivist closed her eyes.

"This is not a disposal lot," she said.

"No."

"It is also not evidence in any form a court will like."

"Courts are upper-level rooms. I am lower freight."

"That is not a defense."

"It is a location."

She almost smiled, then thought better of it.

Together they wrote the intake addendum. Not cleanly. Clean records were too easy
for hostile systems to smooth. They wrote redundantly, with count, witness, chalk
marks, bay number, worker names, the board's refusals, the coherence change, and
the observed zero-memory residue described as observation, not conclusion.

The personal-effects owner field remained.

The novice tried three times to transfer it to Archivist custody. Each time the
system flashed:

SOURCE CATEGORY: NO CARGO.

Then it cleared the lot.

Each time, Toma's name brought it back.

OWNER: TOMA VEY.

ITEM COUNT: 17.

He watched the novice understand.

"It believes you more than it believes the lot," she said.

"It believes I can be punished."

"Yes."

"Do you need me to keep it?"

She did not answer quickly enough to be cruel.

That was answer enough.

Toma rested both hands on the slate. The bay around him felt thick now, not with
stability but with attention. Workers at the stripe. An Archivist witness. Chalk
numbers. His own name tied like a poor rope around seventeen units of not-cargo.

The rope would fray. He could feel it already. His employee profile had dropped
his middle initial. His residence access wanted local verification. The slate's
corner no longer showed his shift history before the current year.

The absence had found a place to press.

He thought of his aunt in the bakery storage room, flour on her sleeve.

Hands count what will be missed.

He wondered, suddenly, whether he had ever told anyone she taught him that. The
memory felt private. Not protected. Those were different things.

"Keep it under me," he said.

The Archivist's mouth tightened. "Until I can get a senior seal."

"Until then."

"This may affect your record."

"It already has."

"More than lunch."

"I know."

He did not know. Not fully. Knowing a cost before paying it was a luxury pilots
pretended at and clerks envied from a distance.

The novice set the witness stone on the nearest container.

"Toma Vey," she said, formal now. "Do you attest that seventeen sealed containers
entered Bay Twelve under Lot HN-0-7741, that the manifest denied cargo, that local
systems repeatedly reduced count, and that you attached the lot to your identity
to prevent administrative erasure?"

"Yes."

"Do you attest without claiming knowledge of contents, origin beyond available
route sleeve, or responsible party?"

"Yes."

"Do you understand this does not make you owner in moral fact?"

That one almost undid him.

The bay waited. The workers waited. Somewhere above, upper levels moved heavy with
names that did not have to fight for fields.

"Yes," he said.

The witness stone brightened.

The containers became easier to see.

Not safe. Not solved. Only present.

## VI. Restricted Hold

They moved the lot near dawn.

By then Bay Twelve had remembered its name, though it kept looking embarrassed
about it. The chalk marks survived under boot dust. The intake board displayed
the count with a caution bracket.

LOT HN-0-7741. PERSONAL EFFECTS HOLD. OWNER: TOMA VEY. COUNT: 17. ARCHIVIST
WITNESS PENDING SENIOR SEAL.

The phrase personal effects looked obscene beside the white containers. Toma left
it. Obscenity was sometimes the record telling the truth by accident.

Restricted custody arrived with six upper-deck officers, two disposal handlers,
and a supervisor who used Toma's full name without looking at his face. The
Archivist novice made them count aloud before each unit crossed the bay stripe.
The lead handler counted louder than necessary. The old recycler counted with his
eyes closed. The food supervisor counted like someone signing a debt she had not
wanted and would not disown.

Seventeen entered the restricted lift.

Seventeen left Bay Twelve.

The lift doors closed.

The intake board blinked.

COUNT: 0.

Toma stepped toward it, but the Archivist caught his sleeve.

"Wait."

The board blinked again.

COUNT TRANSFERRED.

Then, in smaller letters:

OWNER RECORD RETAINED.

Toma let out a breath. It did not feel like relief. Relief implied weight leaving.
This was weight moving somewhere less visible.

His slate chimed.

Residence access suspended pending identity review.

Meal allocation remained unavailable. Shift history displayed only the current
watch. Under family contact, the system showed a blank line where his aunt's bakery
district should have been. Not gone from him. Not yet. But the record no longer
helped hold it.

The Archivist read the notice because his slate was still in witness mode.

"I can write a temporary credential," she said.

"Will it hold?"

"For doors, probably."

"Probably is an upper-deck word for making someone else stand in front of the
door."

This time she did smile. Briefly. Tiredly.

"Yes."

She wrote the credential anyway.

When the bay cleared, Toma picked up the chalk box. He should have sent it back
to stores. Instead he put it in his work bag. Theft, technically. Evidence, if
anyone understood tools.

The lead handler stopped beside him. "You all right?"

"No."

"Good. I would worry if you said yes."

He looked at the empty bay. "Do you remember seventeen?"

"Yes."

"Say it later."

She nodded. "You too."

That was the trouble.

On the walk to his residence block, Heavonly's lower decks shifted into morning
work. Carts moved. Breakfast stalls opened. Recyclers argued over chute priority.
The station was a political hub, a gateway to three galaxies, a bright heavy name
in speeches made far above. Down here it smelled of oil, steamed grain, hot wire,
and people keeping systems honest by touching them.

At his residence door, the reader did not know him.

Toma placed the temporary credential against it. The reader considered, asked for
secondary identity, forgot the question halfway, and asked again.

He could have gone back to freight. Someone there would have let him sleep in a
break room. He could have waited for the Archivist to file the next patch over his
life.

Instead he took chalk from his bag and wrote on the door:

TOMA VEY LIVES HERE.

The letters were ugly. Too large. The kind of statement a child made before
systems learned to be offended.

He wrote beneath it:

SEVENTEEN.

The reader clicked.

Perhaps it accepted the temporary credential. Perhaps the door remembered him.
Perhaps chalk had embarrassed the system into generosity. Toma did not care which
explanation survived review.

The door opened.

Inside, his room was almost as he had left it. Cup on the shelf. Work shirt over
the chair. A repair bill he had meant to ignore until it became urgent. Ordinary
objects with ordinary memory. He touched each one in turn, not ceremonially. He
was too tired for ceremony. He touched them to see whether the room still pushed
back.

The cup did.

The chair did.

The repair bill unfortunately did.

He sat on the bed and tried to remember the bakery storage room. Flour dust. His
aunt's sleeve. Chalk in his hand. Systems count what they are built to count.

The rest answered slowly.

Hands count what will be missed.

Toma held the sentence until it steadied.

Above him, somewhere in Heavonly's restricted levels, seventeen sealed containers
sat in a room that had been forced to admit they existed. Somewhere else, a
quarantined mid-world remained unnamed in the route sleeve. Somewhere far above
both, people with polished voices would decide whether absence could be owned,
disposed of, studied, denied, or sold.

He had not stopped them.

He had made them count around him.

That was not victory. It was inventory.

Before sleeping, Toma opened his slate and checked the lot.

For a moment the screen was blank.

Then, reluctantly, it displayed:

HN-0-7741. COUNT: 17. WITNESS: ACTIVE.

His own employee header flickered above it, lighter than it had been yesterday.

Toma Vey.

The name held.

He set the slate beside the cup, lay back without taking off his boots, and slept
under a door that remembered him in chalk.
