# Spend What You Can Afford

## I. Trays

The quarterly pass began the way the Tessarine Vault liked things to begin: with trays.

Sixteen of them came up from the night porter at first bell, one for each booth in the
audit hall, carried two at a time with both hands the way procedure asked. Ilka Marsk
watched the porter make eight trips. She had been in the hall an hour and had already
filed him under competence. His name was Jory Fenn. He set each tray on its bench without
looking at the Reliquarist who would open it, which was correct, and he did not look at
her, which was also correct. Navigants were not here to be looked at.

She stood at the provenance station at the head of the hall with the seal ledger open
under her palm. The ledger was ceramic and remembered pressure. Three junior seals had
been issued in the quarter. Three was one junior's allotment for a year; across sixteen
booths it was nothing. She read them anyway. Reading them was what the Sect had sent her
to do.

The third seal belonged to a stone numbered TES-N3-44217. Donor Tellis Aru, citizen,
Drift Sleeve Four, seventy-one. Light-bequest to surviving granddaughters. The junior's
margin note read: *Provenance terrain anomalous; possible inherited overlay from donor's
lineage in pre-pedigree window. Stone resonance stable. Memory believes itself.*

Ilka read the word *possible* twice.

Navigants did not care for it. A corridor model that said *possible* was a model no one
had finished. She would pull the stone from the family shelf after the trays were down
and hear it herself. If the terrain was wrong, the pledge would void, and the junior who
had spent a knot to seal it would have spent it for nothing. That was not cruelty. It was
the arithmetic the lintel asked for.

She looked up at the lintel, because she always did.

*Spend only what you can afford. Preserve what must endure.*

Every word was there. The letters were cut deep and darkened by ninety years of hands.

"She has hands," Brother Hael said beside her.

He had come up without her hearing him, which seniors in this hall could do. He was a
broad man with brass-colored burns along both thumbs from a lifetime of cradle arms. He
did not say which junior he meant. He did not have to; the seal was open under her palm.

"Hands are not provenance," Ilka said.

"No." He looked down the hall at the sixteen benches. "They are what you have left when
provenance fails."

She did not answer. At the far end the Vault-Master raised one arm. The quarterly pass
liked the hall to sing at once, and Ilka disliked the custom, and no one had asked her.
Sixteen Reliquarists lowered sixteen rings.

The hall thickened.

It came up through the soles of her boots first, the way it always did in a working hall:
stones giving back their pitches, personal and civic and the long low tone of a family
stone that had been handled for forty years. The sound was not sound. The ear made
it into sound afterward. Ilka let it stand in her teeth and began to count booths by
pitch, because counting was the work.

Booth Nine did not sing.

A silent stone was a corrupted stone. The junior in Nine knew that. Ilka watched her lift
her hands to raise the ring, which was the correct response, and watched the hands stop
above the brass. The junior's face changed. Not fear. Search. She was looking for which
way a ring lifted, and the knowledge was not where she had left it.

Then the partition of Booth Nine was on the wrong side of her.

Ilka saw it happen and did not understand it for one full breath. The booth had an inside
and an outside. The junior sat on the inside. Then the inside was elsewhere, and the
junior's left hand, reaching for the arm of the cradle, went through the brass as if the
brass had forgotten it was between anything.

Booth Three stopped singing.

Booth Eleven.

Booth Fourteen.

Ilka had read the heresy texts because the Sect required it. She had read about lamps
that held no flame, and about the cores the Black Horizon mystics carried into
monasteries, and she had thought of them, when she thought of them at all, as a problem
for the near edge. A cradle gave a stone back its own tone, amplified. She had known that
since her first week in the Orders. She had never once asked what a cradle would give
back if the stone's tone was absence.

"Rings up," she said. It did not come out loud enough. She filled her chest. "Rings up.
Every booth. Now."

Twelve rings went up. Twelve stones that were stones stayed stones.

In Three, Nine, Eleven, and Fourteen the rings did not move, because the Reliquarists
under them could no longer find the motion that moved a ring, and the booths themselves
were becoming places where the eye stopped receiving instruction.

Ilka went down the aisle at a run.

She did not take the junior in Nine by the hand. Hands were the first thing a booth like
that would take. She got both fists into the back of the girl's robe and pulled, and her
own left sleeve passed through the booth partition on the way out, cloth through brass,
with no resistance at all and a cold that went to the elbow. She kept pulling. The junior
came out onto the aisle floor on her knees.

"Your name," Ilka said.

The girl said it. Ilka would write it down later. The girl held her hands up in front of
her face and looked at them. They were whole.

"They're there," the junior said. "I can see them. I can't find them."

"Sit on them."

The girl sat on her hands. That gave the floor a fact to hold and the hands a place to be.

Hael was already moving the hall. He did not shout. He said, "Juniors to the lintel.
Seniors, hold your booths," and the hall obeyed him because it had obeyed him for thirty
years. Four seniors pulled three more people out of Three and Eleven and Fourteen. Two
came out whole. One came out without the memory of his cradle hand, and he would not tune
again. The seniors who stayed put both palms flat on their benches and began to say the
hall to itself. *Sixteen booths. Sixth-generation brass. Resonance plate, two arms, fork
for cross-check.* They were not Continuity Clerics. Every Archivist knew how to recite a
room. The recitation ran down the aisle like a rail being laid.

It held the twelve.

The four places had edges, and the edges moved. The senior in Booth Ten, beside Nine,
began to lose the rail he was holding; Ilka saw his knuckles whiten on a thing that was
becoming less of a thing. Hael stepped into the aisle beside him and said the hall
louder, and the rail came back under the man's hand with the small sound a rail makes
when it remembers it is for holding.

The recitation did not reach the four. Those stayed what they had become: four places in
a working hall where the shelving simply declined to continue, and where a stone sat on
something that was no longer a cradle, giving absence back to absence.

Ilka stood and looked for the porter.

Jory Fenn was not in the hall.

The service door at the head of the stair stood open. On the first landing, set down as
neatly as a tray, was a lamp. It held no flame. It gave a hush.

She understood the whole shape then, the way a Navigant understands a bad model all at
once when the last line goes in. The hall was not the target. The hall was the
detonation. The hall was where every senior in the Vault would be at first bell on the
quarterly pass with their rings down. Above the hall was the middle ring, shelf on shelf
of sealed pledges. Above the ring were the silos.

"Hael," she said.

He was beside her. He had seen the lamp.

"How many porters signed in with the week's trays?"

"Two couriers. Fenn signed them." His face did not change. "He has been here five months."

"Keep the recitation on the twelve. When they hold, get everyone out through the lintel
doors and seal the hall."

"The four stones."

"Stay where they are. No one goes where the booths were."

She went to the stair. Hael gave the hall to the senior in Ten with four words and came
behind her.

## II. The Stair

The lamp on the landing was the size of a cupped hand: a gray housing, a shutter of darker
gray that did not quite close over the core. Around it the stair had begun to lose its
count.

Ilka saw the steps forget their numbers before they forgot anything else. The landing she
stood on was the first landing. It was also, from a slightly different angle, the second.
The riser under her right boot was a riser and was also the idea of a riser, and the idea
was thinning.

Meaning first. Then orientation. Then the material would stop agreeing to be stood on.

She knew what the texts said about null cores: that they worked where they were not
attended; that forgetting was neglect made physical; that the Synod carried them without
harm because the Synod had already put down most of what a core could take. She knew what
the Sect taught about attention, because the Sect taught nothing else. Navigants learned
attention-binding on dead corridors, models with no ship in them, so that they would know
what a pilot's mind was spending before they were asked to judge it.

No one had ever asked her to spend it herself.

She picked up the lamp with her left hand.

The cold did not arrive. It was already there, the way a pressure is already there when
you notice it. Her hand was on the housing. Her hand was holding the housing. She could
see the fingers close. Then the fingers belonged to the lamp more than they belonged to
her, and she understood that the transfer had happened before she could have decided
anything about it.

She gave the lamp her attention anyway. All of it.

Not the way one looks at a thing. The way a pilot holds a corridor mouth that has begun
to pucker: by refusing to let the mind leave it. She made the lamp the only object in the
Vault. The stair, which she was not attending to, remembered its count. The second landing
agreed to be the second landing. Under her boots the risers grew edges.

She climbed.

Hael came behind her. On the fourth step he said, "Navigant, the ring is—"

Her attention moved to his voice by a hair. The step under his boot forgot to be a step
and his leg went through it to the shin. He did not cry out. He caught the rail with both
hands and pulled the leg back, and the step was a step again under him and showed nothing.

"Do not speak to me while I hold this," she said.

He did not speak again until the ring.

The middle ring of the Tessarine Vault was a circle of shelving under a low ceiling, lit
from below so the stones on the shelves threw their small warmths upward. Pledges waited
here after audit, for hours or for weeks: family stones awaiting draw, personal pledges
awaiting silo transfer, light-bequests bedded in felt until the hearer was of age. On an
ordinary day it was the gentlest room in the building.

Two shelves on the inner curve had gone silent.

Not quiet. Silent with structure, the way Booth Nine had become. The acolytes had passed
this way with their lamps, and the stones on those two shelves had given back absence to
a room that had not been reciting anything, and now they were small gray carriers that
had once been a grandmother's morning, a father's first boat, a man's pride in a tool he
had made. Forty stones. Ilka counted because counting was the work, and because someone
would ask.

Between the silent shelves and the stair, the ring floor had begun to lose its seams.

Inya Solven stood at the far curve with the ring ledger open against her chest.

She was small for the robe and very still. Ilka knew her face from the seal file, which
carried a face because the Sect liked to know what it was judging. The junior's eyes went
to the lamp in Ilka's left hand, then to Ilka's face, and stayed there.

"The hall?" she said.

"Four booths. Sealed." Ilka walked the outer curve, where the floor still knew what it
was. The junior had already moved to it; Ilka noted that too. "Every stone on these
shelves. Into trays. Up to the silos."

Inya did not move. "They're pledged."

"I know what they are."

"They're personal. A silo won't take personal weight. They're too small."

Hael spoke then, quietly, from behind. "Not alone."

Inya looked at him. Something went across her face that Ilka did not have time to read.

"Trays," Ilka said. "Two hands. Bring two juniors who still have theirs."

She left them in the ring and took the last flight with the lamp.

## III. Nine Housings

The silo bank was the heaviest room in the Vault, and Ilka's knees knew it before her eyes
did.

Nine housings stood in a shallow arc, each the height of a standing man, brass over
ceramic, each with a tuning aperture at chest height and a pitch you did not hear so much
as carry in the roots of your teeth. Three generations of the Helios Drift's outer sleeve
rested here. Not the memory of any one person. The memory of what a sleeve had agreed to
be: its founding, its routes, its reasons for staying heavy enough to hold stations and
tethers and the small pods that clung to its edges. Every housing was tuned once a year.
Every housing was, this morning, the only thing standing between more than a quarter of a
million people and the slow forgetting of their own floors.

The upper doors stood open for the review, as they stood open four mornings a year. Fenn
had needed only one of them.

Two acolytes in porter gray were setting lamps against the housings.

They did it without hurry. One lamp stood at the foot of the Second Sleeve housing. One at
the Fifth. One at the Seventh. One at the Ninth. A fifth had been set, earlier, against
the Fourth, and Ilka knew it before she looked, because the arc was missing a tooth.

The Fourth Sleeve housing stood where it had always stood. It gave back nothing. Its pitch
was not low or wrong or thin. It was absent, with the shape of a pitch around the absence,
and the brass was brass and the ceramic was ceramic, and three generations of a sleeve
were not inside it anymore.

Inya, coming up the stair behind her with the first tray, said, "Sleeve Four."

Then, lower: "The granddaughters."

Ilka had read the pledge slip. She did not ask.

Jory Fenn stood at the Second Sleeve housing with a lamp in both hands, held against the
brass at the height of the aperture. He looked at Ilka with the courtesy of a porter who
has been asked a question in a hallway.

"Navigant," he said.

Behind Ilka the Vault-Master came off the stair with five porters, breathing hard. He saw
the arc. He saw the missing tooth. He said nothing, which Ilka would remember in his
favor.

Hael went for the nearest acolyte.

He did not go for the lamp. He went for the man, shoulder first, with thirty years of a
broad body behind it, and the acolyte went over sideways, and the lamp struck the deck and
split along its shutter. The core rolled free. It was smaller than the housing had made it
look, a gray thing like a river stone, and where it rolled the deck stopped agreeing to be
a deck.

The acolyte got to his knees on the place where it had rolled.

Ilka watched him lose the floor in the order the texts promised. First his kneeling lost
its meaning: he knelt as if kneeling were a question he had been asked in a language he
was still learning. Then his orientation went: he leaned into a down the room did not
share, slowly, like a man listening for something below him. Then the deck did not hold.
He went through it without a sound, as cloth goes through brass, and the ring below
received him from a height that was not survivable.

The deck closed over the place. It did not remember the hole. There was no mark.

"He is lighter now," Jory Fenn said.

Not to anyone. It was the kind of thing a man says when a fact arrives and he has been
trained to greet it.

The second acolyte finished setting his lamp against the Seventh housing and stepped back
from it and let the porters take him by the arms. He did not resist. His work was placed.
There was nothing left in him that needed to be anywhere.

"Put it down," Ilka said to Fenn.

"You are asking me to interrupt a mercy."

"I am telling you."

He looked at her left hand on the stair lamp. Something in his face approved of what he
saw there, and that was worse than contempt would have been. Then he set his lamp against
the brass of the Second Sleeve housing, where he had been holding it, and stepped back,
and sat down on the deck with his hands open on his knees, and was done.

The porters took him.

"Vault-Master," Ilka said.

"Navigant."

"Five lamps against five housings and one core bare on the deck. I need six pairs of
hands. Each of you takes one. Both hands. Look at it. Do not stop looking at it. Do not
speak, and do not let anyone speak to you. It will take your hands. It has taken mine. The
housings matter more than your hands."

The Vault-Master looked at his five porters. Then he knelt where the acolyte had gone
through and picked up the bare core with both hands and looked at it, and Ilka watched his
fingers become something that belonged to the stone. When he tried to stand he did not, for
a moment, seem to know how a man stands. Then he stood.

The porters took the lamps off the brass. One of them wept while he did it and kept looking.

The deck where the core had lain remained a question.

The Vault-Master's slate, hooked at his belt, lit with the Canticle channel. He could not
take it; his hands were spoken for. Ilka took it with her right.

"Vault, this is the Canticle cutter at your lower dock." A young man's voice, steady, with
the flatness of someone who had learned to report before he learned to be afraid. "Drift
control is calling thinning on every outer-sleeve mouth. Halix reports two decks gone to
full recitation. I have two pods on the tether lines calling in corners. I need to know
what is happening above me."

"Pilot," Ilka said. "This is Navigant Marsk. The silos are under null pressure. One is
lost. Four are losing pitch. Stay on your drive and keep your crew aboard. If the sleeve
goes soft, yours is the only mouth we have."

A pause on the channel. "Understood. Pilot Ren, holding."

She hooked the slate back on the Vault-Master's belt, because he could not, and turned to
the arc.

Four housings losing pitch. The Second Sleeve, the Fifth, the Seventh, the Ninth. The
lamps were off them. The pressure the lamps had made was not. A null did not leave a room
when you lifted it any more than a cold left a room when you closed the door. It had
already been in the walls.

The first trays came up the stair.

## IV. Afford

Hael showed them how a silo took weight.

The tuning aperture of the Second Sleeve housing opened under his burned thumbs like a
mouth that had not been asked to eat in a year. He set a pledged stone in it, a small one,
polished pale by someone's pocket. The silo drank it. The housing's pitch steadied by a
degree Ilka felt in one back tooth, and then slipped again.

"Personal won't bind civic," Hael said. "It thickens. It doesn't hold."

"Then thicken it until something can hold."

Inya set the tray down on the deck between them. There were forty stones on it, bedded in
felt, each with its pledge slip tucked under. The two juniors behind her carried forty
more each. The ring had held perhaps two hundred.

Four housings. Two hundred small warmths. Ilka did the arithmetic because arithmetic was
the only honest thing in the room.

"Vault-Master. Who lives in the Second Sleeve?"

He could not look away from the core. He answered the deck in front of it. "Eleven
stations and the city-ship. Two hundred thousand."

"Fifth."

"Eighty thousand."

"Seventh."

"Forty. A little more."

"Ninth."

The pause was not hesitation. It was a man checking a number he already knew.

"Four stations. Nine hundred. Most of them old."

Ilka looked at the Ninth Sleeve housing. Its pitch was going the way the Fourth's had gone:
not down, not thin, but toward a silence with the shape of a sound around it.

"Let it go," she said.

"Navigant."

"Nine hundred. Say it."

"Nine hundred."

"Then the ring goes to the other three. Write that it was mine."

"That is not yours to spend."

"No," Ilka said. "Write that down too."

They worked.

Inya fed the Second Sleeve housing. She did it the way she must have done everything in
the hall, two hands, no hurry she could not justify, and as each stone went into the
aperture she read the slip under it aloud. Not the number. The name. "Orsin Vell, Sleeve
Two, personal." The silo drank. "Mab Tarrow, Sleeve Five, light-bequest." The silo drank.
Ilka understood that the junior was making a Loss Ledger with her mouth because there was
no slate in the room with a field for this, and she did not stop her.

The two juniors fed the Fifth and the Seventh. Hael moved between all three housings with
his palms, reading pitch through the brass the way a tuner reads a plate.

The Ninth went while they worked.

Ilka made herself listen. She had released it. The least she owed it was to hear it stop.
Its pitch came down to the place where a pitch becomes the memory of one, and then it was
not a memory either, and the housing stood in the arc as brass and ceramic with nothing in
it that four stations and nine hundred old people had agreed to be. Somewhere beyond the
Vault's skin, in the thin outer dark, four stations began very slowly to lose their
reasons.

The Vault felt it go.

The door of the silo bank, behind them, forgot which way it opened and stood half in both.
A seam in the deck under the empty trays blurred into the deck beside it. The Vault sat in
the outer sleeve too. It had always held itself by what it held.

The slate on the Vault-Master's belt lit again. Ilka took it.

"Vault, the dock clamps are asking what they're for." Pilot Ren's voice had not changed.
"I can hold her on thrust. Not for long."

"Hold."

"Holding."

The Fifth steadied first. Then the Seventh. Not well. Not whole. The pitches held at a
depth Ilka would have failed in a review and would now sign for.

The Second did not.

It was the largest silo and the oldest, and it had taken the most lamp. It drank stone
after stone and thickened and would not bind. Inya reached the bottom of the second tray
and lifted the next stone and stopped with it in her palm.

Ilka saw which one it was before Inya's face told her. A stone the size of a finger joint,
polished by a thumb on a chain for years. TES-N3-44217. It was the only stone on the tray
still warm. In a room where every pledge gave back less than it had, this one gave back
what it had always given: a clean middle pitch, salt and wet rope under it, a harbor that
had been scoured before the woman who remembered it was born.

The junior put it in her sleeve.

Ilka looked at her. The junior looked back. Neither of them said what the doctrine said.

"Log it," Ilka said.

"Yes, Navigant."

Inya fed the next stone and read its name.

The third tray emptied into the Second Sleeve. The pitch rose and slipped and rose and
slipped, and the housing trembled under Hael's palms like a thing deciding.

He took his hands off the brass. He looked at the empty trays. He looked at Ilka.

Then he put his hands back on the housing, bare, and Ilka understood what he was going to
do one full breath before he did it, and the breath was the whole of her office.

"Hael."

"Stones don't attend," he said. Not to her. To the brass. "I do."

Then: "The first cradle I tuned. The one with the cracked plate. It sang a quarter flat
and I loved it."

He gave it.

The silo took it the way it had taken no stone all morning. The pitch rose by a clean
degree and held there, and the room got heavier by the exact weight of a cracked plate a
young man had loved, and Hael got lighter by the same.

Ilka had the sentence in her mouth. She had been trained for the sentence. The Navigant
Sect existed, at the end of every argument, to say it.

She looked at the slate hooked on the Vault-Master's belt. Two hundred thousand.

"Name them as they go," she said instead.

Hael named them.

"The night bell, before they changed it. Three strokes and the third one late."

The silo drank.

"My father's cough from the next room. I was nine. It meant he was alive."

The silo drank. Ilka watched the Vault-Master's eyes come off the core and go to Hael and
slide off, and come back to the core. The porters, who were looking at their lamps as they
had been told, did not look up, and so did not see the way the room had begun to let the
man at the housing go.

"A name," Hael said. "I will not say which."

He gave that too.

Inya had stopped reading the slips. She stood with an empty tray against her chest and her
mouth open on a word that would not come out of it.

"Hael, stop," she said.

He turned his head. The room had already begun to forget him, and so what Ilka saw in his
face she saw the way one sees a thing at the edge of the eye, by refusing to look straight
at it.

"Hands, Inya," he said.

He gave the Vault-Master's voice reading the lintel over him on the morning of his
ordination. He gave the first clean pitch he had ever heard under a ring, which he had kept
for forty years as the reason for everything after it.

He gave the seventh.

Ilka did not hear what it was. Neither, afterward, did he.

The Second Sleeve housing bound.

The pitch did not rise. It settled, the way a thing settles when it has found the ground,
and stayed, and the room took the weight of eleven stations and a city-ship and two
hundred thousand people's agreement to remain a sleeve. Ilka felt it in her knees and in
her spine and in the one back tooth that had been counting all morning. Behind her the
door remembered its hinge. The seam in the deck closed.

Hael stood with both palms flat on the brass.

The room let go of him.

Not his body. His body stood there, broad, burned at the thumbs, breathing. His face was a
face. But the room would not hold him. Ilka's eyes went to him and were given nothing to
rest on and moved to the housing. She brought them back by force. They slid again. The
porters had not noticed there had ever been a man there. The Vault-Master kept looking at
the core in his hands, and his mouth moved on a word, and gave up.

Inya said, "Hael."

The name went out into the room and fell and did not land on anything.

She said it again. It did the same.

Ilka took the stylus from her sleeve with her right hand, because her left was still the
lamp's, and pressed it to the brass of the Second Sleeve housing at the height of a
standing man's eyes, and wrote HAEL into the metal hard enough to score it.

The room held the letters.

That was all it would hold.

## V. Endure

The porters carried the lamps down to the lower dock in a line, each man looking at his
own. The Vault-Master came after them between two juniors, with the bare core in both hands
and his eyes on it, and let the juniors tell his feet where the stairs were.

Ilka walked behind them with the stair lamp in her left hand, which she had not put down in
two hours because there was nowhere in the Vault to put it down. Her hand went where she
sent it. It did what she told it. When she did not look at it, it was not hers; she had to
glance down to know it had closed. The texts called this transfer. The Reliquarists called
it softening. She would call it, in the report, what it was.

The Canticle cutter sat in the dock with its lock open and its drive at the low idle of a
ship that had been told to be ready for two hours. The clamps held it. The pilot stood in
the lock. He was younger than his voice. He looked at the six lamps and the bare stone in
seven pairs of hands, and at the hands, and then at hers.

"Pilot Ren."

"Navigant." He did not step aside. "I carry what is on the manifest. All of it named. I
don't carry what isn't."

She took the manifest slate from his hand with her right and wrote.

*Null stones, Synod make, seven: six housed, one bare. Attended in transit.*

"Attended by whom," he said.

"You."

He read it. He did not argue the word. "Then write what I expect to spend."

"You don't know what you'll spend."

"I know it isn't nothing." He looked at her hand. "Write that it isn't nothing."

She wrote: *Expected pilot expenditure: not negligible. To be named post-run.*

He nodded once and took the slate back and read the line again, as if making sure it would
stay.

"What did it take from you?" he asked.

"My hand."

"Which?"

"I have to look to know."

He did not touch the lamps. He opened the reliquary hold where the Vault's sealed crates
would have ridden on an ordinary day and stood aside, and the porters carried their lamps
in one at a time and set them down and backed out still looking at them. Ilka set the
stair lamp down among them with the hand that was not hers, and had to look to see the
fingers open. The Vault-Master went in last with the bare core and set it in the center of
the hold and stood over it until a junior led him out by the sleeve. Pilot Ren closed the hold on the seven of them
and stood a moment with his palm flat on the door.

"Drift control has the Second Sleeve mouths thickening," he said. "Fifth and Seventh thin,
holding. I'm not getting anything from the Ninth."

"No," Ilka said. "You won't."

"The Navigant house at the inner relay. Two pulls. I'll file what it costs."

"File it under your name. Not mine."

"Yes, Navigant."

The lock closed.

She went back up.

The audit hall was sealed. The seniors had recited it steady and come out through the
lintel doors, and the senior from Booth Ten had sealed it behind them. Behind the door
the twelve good booths stood in a hall the seniors had recited back into itself, and the
four bad ones stood where no one would go, with four stones on four things that were not
cradles, giving back absence. No pledge would be audited at Tessarine until someone decided
how to walk into a place that had forgotten it was a place. Across the outer sleeve, forty
families a week would be told to wait. Some of them would not have the time.

Jory Fenn sat in a storeroom off the ring with a porter at the door. The porter was looking
at his hands. Fenn was not looking at anything.

Ilka stood in the doorway.

"You held one," he said. "Now you know what it costs to keep."

"I know what it cost my hand."

"The Drift pays that with every generation it is made to carry. The silos are not memory.
They are weight set on people who did not choose it, so that the people before them can go
on being true." He said it gently. He believed it the way the lintel believed itself. "We
were ending the payment."

"Two hundred thousand people in the Second Sleeve did not ask you to end it."

"They did not ask to be held, either. No one does. That is the whole argument."

"It is not an argument," Ilka said. "It is a count."

She left him for the Inquisition.

In the silo bank, Inya Solven sat on the deck beside the Second Sleeve housing with her
back to the brass. Hael stood where he had stood. No one had moved him. No one, Ilka
understood, had been able to keep in mind that there was anyone to move. The junior had her
stylus out, and every few breaths she went over the letters Ilka had scored into the metal,
deepening them, keeping them dark.

"Hael," Inya said, to the room. Then, after a count of breaths, "Hael."

The name did not land. She said it anyway. She had been taught how to recite a hull.

Ilka crouched beside her. Her knees complained of the room's new weight.

"The stone in your sleeve," she said.

"It's the only one left in the ring." Inya did not take it out. "It held. Everything else
gave back less. It gave back what it always gave."

"That is not an explanation."

"No, Navigant."

"Your review of it is deferred. Write that it held."

"Yes, Navigant."

Ilka looked at the man at the housing, and could not, and looked at the letters instead.

"You have a knot of him," she said. It was not in any file. It was in the way the junior
had said his name. "Does it answer?"

"Yes." Inya's hand went still on the stylus. "He says *hands*. It still answers. It points
at him." She looked at the place where Hael stood, and her eyes went off him the way
everyone's did. "It points at a man the room won't hold."

Ilka had nothing in doctrine for that. She did not pretend to.

She left the junior saying the name and went down to the head of the hall, to the sealed
door and the provenance station outside it, where the seal ledger still lay open under the
lintel with three junior seals in it. She pulled a clean slate from the station drawer with
her right hand. She set the lamp hand flat on the bench where she could see it.

She wrote.

*Tessarine Vault, quarterly pass. Four of sixteen review trays carried null stones of
Synod make, slotted by the night porter over five months. Cradled, the stones were
amplified by the booths. Audit hall: four booths lost; hall sealed with the four stones
inside; pledge audit suspended across the outer sleeve. Middle ring: forty pledged stones
lost to null pressure; remaining pledged stones, approximately one hundred sixty, spent
into silo housings on this Navigant's order, without donor consent. Silos: Fourth Sleeve
lost before defense. Ninth Sleeve released by this Navigant's triage; nine hundred living.
Fifth and Seventh held at reduced pitch. Second Sleeve held. Second Sleeve bound by
expenditure of senior Reliquarist Hael's anchor sequence, complete. Final expenditure
classified by this Navigant as zero-anchoring. Not prevented. Reliquarist Hael:
catastrophic loss; present in body; not held by the room. Recommend Solace. One Synod
agent dead by floor loss. Two in custody for the Inquisition. Eleven null stones total: four sealed in the
hall, seven in Canticle custody, attended; cost to be filed by the pilot. One pledged stone, TES-N3-44217, withheld
from expenditure by junior Reliquarist Solven; held its pitch under null pressure; review
deferred. One junior and one senior: hands. Five porters: hands. Vault-Master: both hands, bare
core; does not stand without help. Navigant Marsk: left hand, transfer, permanent.*

She read it back. It was accurate. Accuracy was the part the Sect permitted.

She looked up at the lintel, because she always did.

*Spend what you can afford. Preserve what must endure.*

She read it twice.

The second word had been *only*. The stone where it had been was not scraped. There was no
mark of a tool. It was shallow, the way a place is shallow after it has forgotten what it
held. The letters on either side were as deep as ever, and the hands of ninety years had
darkened them, and between them was a smoothness the length of one word.

A new carving would be a new line. The Vault would not remember it as the old one. Nothing
the Sect knew could put the word back where it had been.

Ilka wrote it into the report, after *Spend*, where it belonged, and signed under it.
